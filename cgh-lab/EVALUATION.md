# Trying cGħ World on people

A guide for running short sessions with classmates, so you learn whether the world builds understanding and
not only enthusiasm. The page has everything needed: missions that record predictions, and a ten-question
quiz taken before and after.

## What a session can and cannot show

A handful of sessions can show you what confuses people, which missions change their answers, and where the
interface gets in the way. It cannot prove the world teaches better than a lecture: for that you need a
comparison group, more people, and questions they have not seen before. Say so when you report results.

## Plan for one person (60–75 minutes)

| Minutes | Step | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Welcome and consent | Explain the purpose, that it is not a test of them, that they may stop at any time, and what you will record. Use first names or codes only. |
| 8 | “Before” quiz | In the page: *Check yourself → Take the “before” quiz*. No help. |
| 5 | Free play | Let them move, turn a dial, press a preset. Note what they try first. |
| 35 | Missions | Pick five or six, in this order: 1 Lamps, 2 Twin trip, 3 Colours from the tower, 6 Two doors, 7 The bell, 8 Look closer. Add 9 Satellite or 15 Two staircases if time allows. |
| 8 | “After” quiz | Same questions. |
| 10 | Short interview | See the questions below. |
| 2 | Copy results | *Copy results* gives quiz scores, the normalised gain and each mission’s prediction. Paste it into your log. |

## During the missions: think aloud

Ask them to say what they expect and what they see, as they go. Prompt only with neutral questions:

- “What do you expect to happen?” (before locking in a prediction)
- “What are you looking at now?”
- “Is that what you expected? Why, or why not?”
- After the explanation: “Can you say it in your own words?”

Do not correct them during a mission. Write down:

| Mission | Prediction and their reason | Surprise? (quote) | Where they got stuck in the interface | Own-words explanation |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Interview questions

1. Which moment changed how you think about something? What changed?
2. Was anything confusing about what you were seeing, as opposed to the physics?
3. Did you trust what you saw? When did you suspect a bug? (Students using an earlier relativity simulator
   sometimes blamed real effects on bugs: McGrath et al. 2010.)
4. What would you tell a friend measurement means, in quantum mechanics?

## Analysing the results

- **Normalised gain** for each person: (after − before) / (10 − before), the share of the questions they
  missed before that they now get right (Hake 1998). The page computes it.
- **Per question:** count how many people moved from wrong to right, and from right to wrong. Questions
  where people move the wrong way point to a mission that misleads.
- **Predictions:** the mission log shows which predictions were wrong before the explanation. A mission
  where almost everyone predicts right is not teaching anything new; one where almost everyone is wrong
  and stays wrong in the quiz needs a better explanation.
- **Interface problems** from your notes are the cheapest things to fix. Fix them before the next round.

## Threats to what you conclude

- The quiz uses the same questions before and after; some gain is just familiarity.
- Novelty: a game is more engaging than a lecture whether or not it teaches more.
- Your classmates are not typical learners, and you know them.
- Ten questions is a small instrument. For stronger claims use research-based ones: the Quantum Mechanics
  Conceptual Survey (McKagan, Perkins and Wieman 2010), the Relativity Concept Inventory (Aslanides and Savage
  2013), or a recent questionnaire on measurement in two-state systems
  ([Bitzenbauer 2024](https://doaj.org/article/8e8dbaf6be6844fb973958b2a5f103b2)). Check each one's terms of
  use before using it; the first two are cited from memory.

## After two or three rounds

Write down: what you changed and why, the gains before and after each change, and the quotes that
best show a change of mind. That is enough for a short report, a poster, or a conversation with a lecturer
about trying it in a tutorial.
