Walk round a small planet

The planet is 382 m across, with one street running round it. Every pixel is a ray of light traced back through curved spacetime to whatever sent it, with the time it took. You start facing a clock tower; behind you is a wall with two doors.

What each dial does to the view

  • c Moving squeezes the view ahead, tints it blue and brightens it; behind you it reddens and fades. Far clocks read early because their light is old.
  • G Light falls. The horizon rises, the street ahead curls up, and you see further round the planet. At 2/3 light circles the planet at eye level; beyond that the ground wraps over you and the sky shrinks to a blue disc overhead. The tower-top clock runs fast.
  • ħ You become a wave. Left alone you spread, and the view overlays every place you might be. Walls reflect you; two doors split you. Looking (F) puts you in one place, at random.
  • ħ Light arrives in lumps. With few photons per pixel, the scene turns to grain.

What to try

  • Press “Light orbits”, look along the street, then look straight up.
  • Press “You are a wave”, walk at the wall between the doors, and watch the map. Press F on the far side, several times from the start.
  • Turn on “Always watched”. Being looked at keeps you sharp, but you jitter: each look kicks your momentum.
  • Tick “Newton’s view” to see the same place with instant, straight light.

What is exact, and what is not

Light paths are exact null geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric outside the planet, traced per pixel, with their travel time, gravitational and Doppler shifts. Your own wave obeys the free relativistic wave equation for a spinless particle in two dimensions, with walls. Approximations: colours (a guessed spectrum), sunlight (not bent), the overlay (an average of views, which no single observer sees), and a planet whose mass is set by decree.

Why pure numbers

Only ratios without units are physical. Each dial is one: your walking speed against light, the planet’s horizon radius against its size, your wavelength against a metre, and the photons that reach one pixel. The notes under each dial translate it into c, G and ħ. They also show the catch: the ħ that makes you a 2 m wave would make each photon carry about 3×10³⁵ times its real energy, so a consistent world would be black. The last two dials keep those two effects apart on purpose.
