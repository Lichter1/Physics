Walk round a small planet

The planet is 382 m across, with one street running round it. Every pixel is a ray of light traced back through curved spacetime to whatever sent it, with the time it took. You start facing a clock tower; behind you is a wall with two doors.

What each dial does to the view

  • c Moving squeezes the view ahead, tints it blue and brightens it; behind you it reddens and fades. Far clocks read early because their light is old.
  • G Light falls. The horizon rises, the street ahead curls up, and you see further round the planet. Fly up (E) and lamps below turn red while the sky turns blue. Higher clocks run fast.
  • ħ You become a wave. Left alone you spread, and the view overlays every place you might be. Walls reflect you; two doors split you.
  • ħ Light arrives in lumps. With few photons per pixel, the scene turns to grain.

What to try

  • “Heavy planet”, then “Up to the tower clock”. Point the crosshair at a street lamp below and read the spectrometer.
  • Switch effects off one at a time. Each switch removes exactly one process from the picture.
  • “You are a wave”: walk at the doors until about a third of you is past the wall. Tick the detector screen, look, then “Rewind and look again” forty times. Clear the marks, set the bell to 100%, and repeat.
  • Set the microscope to 0.25 m and look. Watch Δv jump and your spreading speed up.

What a measurement is

  • Outcomes are random, with probability |ψ|². “Rewind and look again” repeats the same experiment on the same wave; the yellow marks pile up where the map is bright. The look did not reveal where you already were: before it, there was no single place.
  • A measurement is any interaction that leaves a record. The bell rings for whoever passes the left door. Nobody reads it, yet the stripes behind the doors fade, because the part of you that went left is now tied to “bell rang”. No conscious observer is needed.
  • Records can be undone, sometimes. “Erase the bell’s record” reads the bell in a way that cannot tell left from right. The stripes come back, shifted for one of the two outcomes. This is the quantum eraser.
  • Measuring disturbs. A sharp look pins your position and so spreads your momentum, because Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2. Watch the meter and how fast you spread afterwards.
  • After a look the state is updated, then evolves again. Look twice quickly: same place. Wait: you spread again. It neither stays collapsed nor snaps back.
  • Why big things look definite: the world looks at them all the time. Set “The world looks at you” to 16 a second: you stay sharp but jitter. Hot molecules lose their stripes the same way, by giving off light.
  • What is not settled is why one outcome happens rather than another. Decoherence explains the fading of stripes; interpretations (Copenhagen, many-worlds, pilot waves, collapse models) differ on the rest. The lab picks no side: “Look” uses the textbook rule.

What is exact, and what is not

Light paths are exact null geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric outside the planet, traced per pixel, with travel time, gravitational and Doppler shifts; the probe re-traces the same ray in JavaScript. Your wave obeys the free relativistic wave equation for a spinless particle in two dimensions, with walls. The bell is a two-state detector coupled to you near the door; looks are Gaussian position measurements. Approximations: colours, unbent sunlight, the overlay of views, and a planet whose mass is set by decree.
