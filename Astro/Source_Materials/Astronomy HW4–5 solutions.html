HW4 · Question 1

Kuiper-belt occultations

When a Kuiper-belt object (KBO) passes between us and a background star, and both are smaller than the Fresnel scale $F=\sqrt{\lambda a/2}$ ($a$ = distance to the Kuiper belt), we get Fraunhofer diffraction. Observing in green light: what size of object can we expect to detect? What is the event's timescale if the dominant velocity is Earth's orbital speed? Extra: where would you point such an experiment, and how would you detect bodies with a range of sizes?

(a) Detectable object size

Findthe size $F$ at which diffraction sets the shadowGivengreen light, Kuiper belt at $a\approx30$–$50$ AU

The Fresnel scale is where geometric shadows give way to diffraction. A body of size $\lesssim F$ still makes a detectable dip, but the dip is about $F$ wide. So $F$ is the characteristic size these events probe.

Convert the inputs to cgs using (C). Green light $\lambda\approx5000$ Å; Kuiper belt at $a\approx40$ AU:

$$\lambda=5000\times10^{-8}=5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm},\qquad a=40\times1.5\times10^{13}=6.0\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{cm}$$

Substitute into (F.1):

$$F=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda a}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{(5\times10^{-5})(6.0\times10^{14})}{2}}=\sqrt{1.5\times10^{10}}=1.2\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{cm}$$

Check how sensitive this is to $a$. $F\propto\sqrt a$, so the 30–50 AU range only gives $F=1.06$–$1.37$ km.

$$\boxed{F\approx1.2\ \mathrm{km}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{objects of order 1 km (sub-km to a few km)}}$$

Why occultations? A 1 km body at 40 AU reflects far too little sunlight to be imaged. Blocking a star is the only way to detect it.

The star must be small too. Its disk, projected onto the Kuiper belt, must be smaller than $F$. Otherwise the pattern is smeared out. That requires an angular diameter $\lt F/a=1.2\times10^5/6\times10^{14}=2\times10^{-10}$ rad $\approx0.04$ mas, which rules out nearby giants and favours distant dwarf stars.

(b) Event timescale

Findduration $t$ of the dipGivenrelative speed $\approx$ Earth's orbital speed

The diffraction shadow is a pattern about $F$ wide lying on the ground. The event lasts as long as Earth takes to sweep across it: $t\approx F/v$.

Earth's orbital speed from (F.2) with $r=1$ AU, $P=1$ yr:

$$v_\oplus=\frac{2\pi(1.5\times10^{13})}{3.15\times10^{7}}=3.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}=30\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$$

Check that Earth really dominates. The KBO's own speed scales as $v\propto r^{-1/2}$ (F.2), so

$$v_{\rm KBO}=\frac{v_\oplus}{\sqrt{40}}=4.7\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\ll v_\oplus$$

Divide size by speed:

$$t\approx\frac{F}{v_\oplus}=\frac{1.2\times10^5}{3.0\times10^6}=0.04\ \mathrm s,\qquad \text{full dip width}\sim\frac{2F}{v}\approx0.08\ \mathrm s$$
$$\boxed{t\sim0.04\text{–}0.08\ \mathrm s\ \ (\text{tens of milliseconds})}$$

Consequence: ordinary exposures of seconds would wash out a 40 ms dip completely. These surveys need high-cadence photometry (≳20–50 frames per second), which is how real KBO occultation searches (TAOS, the HST-FGS search) were designed.

(c) Designing the experiment

Findwhere to point; how to cover many sizesGivenresults of (a), (b)

The event rate is (number of KBOs per area) × (cross-section) × (relative speed) × (number of stars monitored). Maximise each factor.

Point along the ecliptic. KBOs are concentrated within roughly ten degrees of the ecliptic plane, so that is where the "screen" of objects is densest.

Pick ecliptic fields rich in small, faint stars. The ecliptic passes near the Galactic centre (Sagittarius/Scorpius), which gives thousands of background stars per field. The stars should be distant dwarfs, small enough to stay under the 0.04 mas limit from (a).

Choose the viewing geometry for the speed you want. Near opposition Earth's velocity is transverse: $v\approx v_\oplus-v_{\rm KBO}\approx25$ km/s. Events are short but most frequent there (rate $\propto v$). Near quadrature (90° from the Sun) Earth moves along the line of sight, so only $v_{\rm KBO}\approx5$ km/s is transverse. Events there are about 5× longer and easier to resolve.

Covering a range of sizes. Small bodies ($D\lesssim F$) are numerous but give shallow, chromatic diffraction dips about $2F/v$ long. They need fast cadence and high S/N on many stars. Large bodies ($D\gg F$) give deep, flat-bottomed dips of duration $D/v$, which slower cadence can catch. So: monitor as many stars as possible for as long as possible, with fast cadence, in several wavelengths ($F\propto\sqrt\lambda$ changes the fringe pattern, a fingerprint of a real event). Use two or more telescopes a few km apart: a km-scale shadow hits them at different times or with different depths, which confirms the event and measures the size and speed.

$$\boxed{\text{ecliptic, dense star fields, tiny stars; fast multi-band photometry from several sites}}$$

The size distribution of KBOs is steep ($N(\gt D)\propto D^{-q}$ with $q\approx3$–$4$). Counting km-sized bodies this way constrains collisional evolution in the outer Solar System. No other method reaches these sizes.

HW4 · Question 2

Galaxy-scale gravitational lensing

(a) Find the Einstein radius (arcsec and physical units) of a $10^{12}M_\odot$ galaxy lying between us and a source at 1 Gpc, with the lens halfway; treat it as a point mass. (b) How does the answer change if the source is much closer to the lens? If the lens is much closer to us? (c) What happens if the source is slightly off-axis (still inside $\theta_E$)? (d) Galaxies are randomly placed with density $0.01\ \mathrm{Mpc^{-3}}$, all with the mass, distance and Einstein radius above. What fraction of background galaxies at 1 Gpc are lensed by galaxies at half that distance?

(a) Einstein radius

Find$\theta_E$ [″] and $R_E=\theta_ED_l$ [kpc]Given$M=10^{12}M_\odot$, $D_s=1$ Gpc, $D_l=D_{ls}=0.5$ Gpc

A source exactly behind a point lens is imaged into a ring of angular radius $\theta_E$. Simplify the distance factor first, then plug the numbers into (F.3).

Simplify the geometric factor before using any numbers. With $D_l=D_{ls}=D_s/2$:

$$\frac{D_{ls}}{D_lD_s}=\frac{D_s/2}{(D_s/2)\,D_s}=\frac{1}{D_s}$$

The two halves cancel. (Distances are treated as static and Euclidean with $D_{ls}=D_s-D_l$; cosmology is ignored at this level.)

Compute the lens's length scale $4GM/c^2$ with (C), $M=2\times10^{45}$ g:

$$\frac{4GM}{c^2}=\frac{4(6.7\times10^{-8})(2\times10^{45})}{(3\times10^{10})^2}=5.96\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm}\ (\approx0.19\ \mathrm{pc})$$

Substitute into (F.3) with $D_s=10^9\times3.1\times10^{18}=3.1\times10^{27}$ cm:

$$\theta_E^2=\frac{5.96\times10^{17}}{3.1\times10^{27}}=1.92\times10^{-10}\ \Rightarrow\ \theta_E=1.39\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}$$

Convert to arcseconds (1 rad = 206265″):

$$\theta_E=1.39\times10^{-5}\times206265=2.86''$$

Physical radius in the lens plane (small angle):

$$R_E=\theta_ED_l=(1.39\times10^{-5})(1.55\times10^{27})=2.15\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{cm}=6.9\ \mathrm{kpc}$$
$$\boxed{\theta_E\approx2.9'',\qquad R_E\approx7\ \mathrm{kpc}}$$

Sanity check: $R_E\approx7$ kpc is comparable to the visible size of a massive galaxy, so the point-mass assumption is crude but gives the right order of magnitude. Observed galaxy–galaxy lenses do show arcs and rings at about 1–3″.

(b) Moving the lens along the line of sight

Find$\theta_E$ and $R_E$ as the lens position changesGiven$x\equiv D_l/D_s$, $D_{ls}=D_s(1-x)$

Rewrite (F.3) in terms of the single variable $x=D_l/D_s$. Each limit can then be read off directly.

Substitute $D_l=xD_s$, $D_{ls}=(1-x)D_s$:

$$\theta_E=\sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2D_s}\,\frac{1-x}{x}},\qquad R_E=\theta_ED_l=\sqrt{\frac{4GMD_s}{c^2}\,x(1-x)}$$

Source close to the lens ($x\to1$): $(1-x)\to0$, so both $\theta_E\to0$ and $R_E\to0$. The light is bent, but it has almost no distance left in which to move off the original line of sight. The images collapse onto the source and lensing becomes negligible.

Lens close to us ($x\to0$): $\theta_E\propto x^{-1/2}$ grows, so the ring appears larger on the sky. Its physical size $R_E\propto\sqrt x$ shrinks to zero. A nearby lens deflects only a thin tube of light, but we see that tube at a large angle.

Find the most efficient position. $R_E^2\propto x(1-x)$ is maximal at $x=\tfrac12$, which is the configuration of part (a).

$$\boxed{\theta_E\propto\sqrt{\tfrac{1-x}{x}}\ \ (\to0\text{ as }x\to1,\ \to\infty\text{ as }x\to0),\qquad R_E\propto\sqrt{x(1-x)}}$$

The symmetry $x\leftrightarrow1-x$ of $R_E$ reflects the reversibility of light rays: swap the observer and the source and the geometry is the same.

This is also why microlensing by stars inside the Milky Way ($x\ll1$, $M\sim M_\odot$) still gives measurable $\theta_E\sim$ mas, even though $M$ is $10^{12}$ times smaller.

(c) Source slightly off-axis

Findwhat replaces the ringGivensource at angle $\beta\lt\theta_E$ from the lens

The ring exists only because of perfect axial symmetry. Once the source is off-axis, solve the lens equation (F.4) to find where the images go.

Work in units of $\theta_E$ ($u=\beta/\theta_E$, $\theta$ in units of $\theta_E$). (F.4) becomes

$$u=\theta-\frac1\theta\ \xrightarrow{\ \times\theta\ }\ \theta^2-u\theta-1=0$$

Solve the quadratic:

$$\theta_\pm=\frac{u\pm\sqrt{u^2+4}}{2},\qquad \theta_+\theta_-=-1,\quad \theta_++\theta_-=u$$

Read off the geometry. $\theta_+\gt1$: one image outside the ring, on the same side as the source. $\theta_-\lt0$ with $|\theta_-|\lt1$: one image inside the ring, on the opposite side. The ring breaks into two images (two arcs for an extended source).

Example $u=0.5$: $\theta_+=1.28$, $\theta_-=-0.78$. Check: $1.28-1/1.28=0.50$ ✓.

Brightness from (F.5):

$$\mu(0.5)=\frac{0.25+2}{0.5\sqrt{4.25}}=2.18$$

For every $u\lt1$, $\mu\gt1.34$. The images are stretched tangentially by $\theta/\beta$ (surface brightness is conserved), so an extended source becomes two bright arcs hugging the Einstein ring.

$$\boxed{\text{ring}\ \to\ \text{two arcs at }\theta_\pm=\tfrac{\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^2+4\theta_E^2}}{2},\ \text{total }\mu=\tfrac{u^2+2}{u\sqrt{u^2+4}}\gt1.34}$$

A real galaxy is extended and usually elliptical, not a point. An off-axis source then typically produces 4 bright images (an "Einstein cross") plus a faint, demagnified central one. A smooth non-singular lens always gives an odd number of images.

(d) What fraction of sources is lensed?

Findlensing probability (optical depth) $\tau$Given$n=0.01\ \mathrm{Mpc^{-3}}$, $\theta_E=2.86''$ for every lens, $D_s=1000$ Mpc

A source is strongly lensed if it falls inside some foreground galaxy's Einstein disk. The answer is the fraction of the sky (seen from us) covered by those disks.

Count the foreground lenses per steradian. The cone of solid angle $d\Omega$ out to $D_s$ has volume $\int_0^{D_s}r^2dr\,d\Omega=\tfrac13D_s^3\,d\Omega$:

$$N=\frac{nD_s^3}{3}=\frac{0.01\times(1000)^3}{3}=3.3\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{sr^{-1}}$$

Solid angle covered by each lens:

$$\pi\theta_E^2=\pi\times1.92\times10^{-10}=6.0\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{sr}$$

Multiply. Since $\tau\ll1$ the disks practically never overlap, so the covered fraction is just the product:

$$\tau=N\,\pi\theta_E^2=(3.3\times10^{6})(6.0\times10^{-10})=2.0\times10^{-3}$$
$$\boxed{\tau\approx2\times10^{-3}\ \ (\sim1\text{ in }500\text{ sources at 1 Gpc})}$$

Two other readings give the same order: (i) a cylinder of physical radius $R_E$ along the line of sight: $\tau=n\pi R_E^2D_s=0.01\,\pi\,(6.9\times10^{-3})^2(1000)=1.5\times10^{-3}$. (ii) Exact for point lenses, letting $R_E$ vary with position as in (b): $\tau=\int_0^{D_s}n\pi\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{D_l(D_s-D_l)}{D_s}dD_l=n\pi\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{D_s^2}{6}=1.0\times10^{-3}$.

All three land at $\sim10^{-3}$. Strong lensing is rare, which is why lensing surveys must image $\gtrsim10^5$ galaxies to find hundreds of lenses.

HW4 · Question 3 (Maoz Ch. 7, Problem 6)

Flat rotation curves without dark matter

MOND proposes that for small accelerations Newton's 2nd law becomes $F=ma^2/a_0$. (a) Show this leads to flat rotation curves without dark matter. (b) Alternatively, propose a new gravity law replacing $GMm/r^2$ beyond a radius $r_0$ that gives flat rotation curves; check its dimensions. (c) Modify (b) so it is Newtonian for $r\ll r_0$ with a continuous transition to the required behaviour for $r\gg r_0$.

(a) MOND gives $v$ independent of $r$

Find$v(r)$ for a circular orbit under (F.8)Givenmass $M$ (effectively central at large $r$)

In a circular orbit the gravitational force supplies the centripetal acceleration. Replace $F=ma$ with the MOND form and watch $r$ drop out.

Centripetal acceleration (F.6):

$$a=\frac{v^2}{r}$$

Insert it into the MOND law (F.8):

$$F=\frac{ma^2}{a_0}=\frac{m}{a_0}\frac{v^4}{r^2}$$

Set equal to gravity (F.7):

$$\frac{m v^4}{a_0r^2}=\frac{GMm}{r^2}$$

Cancel $m$ (nonzero) and $r^2$ (on both sides). The $r^2$ cancellation is the key: the $1/r^2$ of gravity now matches the $1/r^2$ in $a^2$.

$$v^4=GMa_0$$

Number for a typical galaxy ($M=10^{11}M_\odot$, $a_0=1.2\times10^{-8}$ cm s⁻²):

$$v=\left[(6.7\times10^{-8})(2\times10^{44})(1.2\times10^{-8})\right]^{1/4}=2.0\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}=200\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$$
$$\boxed{v=(GMa_0)^{1/4}\ \ \text{independent of }r\ \Rightarrow\ \text{flat rotation curve}}$$

Free bonus: $M\propto v^4$, so if $L\propto M$ then $L\propto v^4$. That is the Tully–Fisher relation, which MOND predicts rather than fits.

Where MOND switches on: it applies where the Newtonian acceleration $GM/r^2\lt a_0$, i.e. $r\gt\sqrt{GM/a_0}=10.8$ kpc for this galaxy. That is the outer disk, exactly where observed curves are flat.

(b) A modified gravity law beyond $r_0$

Find$F(r)$ for $r\gt r_0$ giving constant $v$Givencircular orbits, $mv^2/r=F$

Work backwards. A flat curve means $v^2/r\propto1/r$, so the force must fall as $1/r$, not $1/r^2$. Build that in with a length $r_0$ so the dimensions still work.

Propose

$$F=\frac{GMm}{r\,r_0}\qquad(r\gt r_0)$$

Check dimensions. $GMm/r^2$ is a force; $r\,r_0$ has dimensions of length², the same as $r^2$, so $[F]$ is unchanged ✓.

Circular orbit: set $mv^2/r=GMm/(r\,r_0)$, then cancel $m$ and one power of $1/r$:

$$v^2=\frac{GM}{r_0}=\text{const}$$

Continuity at $r=r_0$: $GMm/(r_0\cdot r_0)=GMm/r_0^2$, which matches Newton there ✓.

$$\boxed{F=\frac{GMm}{r\,r_0}\ (r\gt r_0)\ \Rightarrow\ v=\sqrt{GM/r_0}}$$

Trap: with one universal $r_0$, $v^2\propto M$, i.e. $L\propto v^2$. That contradicts the observed Tully–Fisher $L\propto v^4$. Making $r_0$ depend on mass as $r_0=\sqrt{GM/a_0}$ gives $v^2=GM/r_0=\sqrt{GMa_0}$, i.e. $v^4=GMa_0$. That reproduces MOND exactly (try the "Tie r₀" preset below).

(c) A smooth Newtonian-to-flat transition

Find$F(r)$: Newtonian for $r\ll r_0$, flat curve for $r\gg r_0$, continuousGivenresult of (b)

Multiply Newton by a dimensionless function $f(r/r_0)$ that is $\to1$ inside and $\to r/r_0$ outside.

Simplest choice $f=1+r/r_0$:

$$F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}\left(1+\frac{r}{r_0}\right)=\frac{GMm}{r^2}+\frac{GMm}{r\,r_0}$$

Circular orbit ($v^2=rF/m$):

$$v^2=\frac{GM}{r}+\frac{GM}{r_0}$$

Check both limits. For $r\ll r_0$ the first term dominates: $v^2\approx GM/r$ (Kepler/Newton). For $r\gg r_0$ the first term dies: $v^2\to GM/r_0$, flat as in (b). Both terms are smooth for $r\gt0$, so the transition is continuous ✓.

Another valid choice with a sharper transition: $f=\sqrt{1+(r/r_0)^2}$ gives $v^2=\frac{GM}{r}\sqrt{1+(r/r_0)^2}$, with the same two limits. Any $f$ with the right limits works, as the question says.

$$\boxed{F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}\left(1+\frac{r}{r_0}\right)\ \Rightarrow\ v^2=\frac{GM}{r}+\frac{GM}{r_0}}$$

Degeneracy with dark matter: the extra term $GMm/(r\,r_0)$ acts like extra mass $M(r/r_0)$ that grows linearly with $r$. That is exactly what an isothermal dark halo ($\rho\propto r^{-2}$, $M\propto r$) provides. Rotation curves alone cannot tell modified gravity from dark matter. Lensing, galaxy clusters and the Bullet Cluster can (see HW5 Q4).

HW5 · Question 1

How do we know other galaxies are outside the Milky Way?

How do we know that the other galaxies we see lie outside the Milky Way?

Distance, size, velocity and sky distribution

Findobservational evidence that the "spiral nebulae" are externalGivenMW disk diameter $\sim30$ kpc, halo $\sim100$ kpc

The question was settled by measuring distances (the 1920 "Great Debate", resolved by Hubble in 1925). Everything else confirms it.

Standard candles give distances. Hubble found Cepheids in M31 and M33. The period gives $L$ (Leavitt's period–luminosity law); the measured flux $f$ then gives $d=\sqrt{L/4\pi f}$ (F.10). He obtained $\sim0.3$ Mpc (the modern value is $0.78$ Mpc). Either way that is $\gg$ the size of the Milky Way.

Size check. M31 spans $\sim3^\circ$ on the sky. At $0.78$ Mpc that is

$$3^\circ\times\frac{\pi}{180}\times780\ \mathrm{kpc}\approx41\ \mathrm{kpc}$$

A system as large as the Milky Way itself, not a small nebula inside it.

Velocities. Galaxy spectra are redshifted. Recession speed grows with distance (F.11), e.g. Virgo at 16.5 Mpc: $v=70\times16.5\approx1150$ km/s. That far exceeds the Milky Way's escape speed ($\sim550$ km/s), so these objects cannot be bound to our Galaxy.

Sky distribution. Objects that belong to the disk (open clusters, H II regions) crowd toward the Galactic plane. Galaxies avoid it (the "zone of avoidance"): we see them through the dust-free poles and lose them behind the dusty disk. So they lie beyond the disk.

Spectra and resolved stars. Galaxy spectra are composite starlight with absorption lines, unlike the emission-line spectra of gaseous nebulae. Deep images resolve them into individual stars that are extremely faint, again implying large distances. Independent candles (novae, brightest stars, the tip of the red-giant branch) all agree.

$$\boxed{d_{\rm galaxies}\gg R_{\rm MW}\ \text{(Cepheid distances), plus sizes, }v\gt v_{\rm esc},\ \text{and avoidance of the plane}}$$

Hubble's 1925 distance was about 3× too small, because the Cepheids he calibrated on were a different type (W Virginis vs classical). Even the too-small number was decisive: what mattered was $d\gg R_{\rm MW}$, not the exact value.

HW5 · Question 2

Galaxy types, their spectra, and spotting an AGN

Briefly summarise the galaxy types and their main differences: stellar population, stellar motions, structure, etc. What differences would you expect in colour or spectrum? How would you distinguish a galaxy with an active massive black hole (AGN) from a "normal" one?

(a) The Hubble sequence and what distinguishes the types

Findcomparison of E, S0, S, IrrGiven—

Organise everything by one idea: how much gas is left and how recently stars formed. That single variable predicts the population, colour and spectrum. The orbits (random vs rotating) set the shape.

TypeStructureStars & gasKinematicsColour / spectrum
E0–E7Smooth ellipsoids, de Vaucouleurs $r^{1/4}$ profile, no disk or armsOld, metal-rich, little cold gas/dust, no star formationPressure-supported: random orbits, velocity dispersion $\sigma\gg v_{\rm rot}$Red; strong absorption lines (Ca H&K, Mg b, Na D), strong 4000 Å break, no emission lines

Why colour tracks type. Massive blue stars live only $\sim10^7$ yr. A galaxy that stopped forming stars loses its blue light quickly and is left with long-lived red giants and dwarfs, so ellipticals are red. Ongoing star formation keeps a blue continuum. The O stars also ionise gas, producing the emission lines.

$$\boxed{\text{E: old, red, absorption, random orbits}\ \to\ \text{S: mixed, rotating disk, emission}\ \to\ \text{Irr: young, blue, strong emission}}$$

The Hubble sequence is not an evolutionary sequence (Hubble's "early/late" terminology is historical). Mergers of disk galaxies can, however, build ellipticals, which is one reason ellipticals are common in dense clusters.

(b) Distinguishing an AGN from a normal galaxy

Findobservational signatures of accretion onto a massive black holeGiven—

Starlight is the sum of thermal (blackbody-like) spectra from many stars. An AGN adds non-stellar light from a tiny central region. Look for what stars cannot produce.

A compact, bright nucleus. A point source at the centre, often outshining the whole galaxy.

Non-thermal continuum. A power law spanning radio to X-rays, much broader than any stellar population: strong UV excess, hard X-rays, sometimes radio jets and lobes.

Broad emission lines (type 1 AGN). Widths of thousands of km/s: gas orbiting deep in the black hole's potential. Normal H II-region lines are $\lesssim100$–$200$ km/s.

High-ionisation narrow lines. Strong [O III] 5007 relative to Hβ, high-ionisation species like [Ne V]. The hard AGN spectrum ionises gas more than O-star light can (separated on the BPT diagram).

Variability. Brightness changes over days. Causality then limits the source size to $R\lesssim c\,\Delta t\sim$ light-days: an enormous luminosity from a tiny volume.

$$\boxed{\text{point nucleus, power-law continuum (radio–X-ray), broad + high-ionisation lines, fast variability}}$$

A type 2 AGN (broad-line region hidden by a dusty torus seen edge-on) has no broad lines. It is identified instead by the line ratios and by X-ray / mid-IR emission from the hidden nucleus.

HW5 · Question 3

Plotting the Schechter luminosity function

The galaxy luminosity distribution is given by the Schechter function $\phi(L)\,dL\approx\phi(L_*)(L/L_*)^{-1}\exp(-L/L_*)\,dL$. Write a short program that plots the number of galaxies as a function of luminosity; choose the normalisation $\phi(L_*)$ freely.

The program and what the plot shows

Findplot of $\phi(L)$Given(F.9), slope $-1$, free $\phi(L_*)$

Work in units of $L_*$ so the program needs no physical numbers, and plot on log–log axes. A power law is then a straight line and the cutoff is easy to see.

Dimensionless form. With $x=L/L_*$:

$$\phi(x)=\phi_*\,x^{-1}e^{-x}$$

The program (Python, numpy + matplotlib):

schechter.py
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

phi_star = 1.0      # normalisation phi(L*) - arbitrary, as the question allows
L_star   = 1.0      # work in units of L*  (L* ~ 2e10 L_sun for real galaxies)

L = np.logspace(-3, 1, 400)                  # 0.001 L* ... 10 L*
phi = phi_star * (L / L_star)**-1 * np.exp(-L / L_star)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.6))
ax[0].loglog(L, phi, lw=2, label=r"Schechter $\phi(L)$")
ax[0].loglog(L, phi_star*(L/L_star)**-1, "--", label=r"power law $L^{-1}$")
ax[0].axvline(L_star, ls=":", c="gray"); ax[0].set_ylim(1e-5, 2e3)
ax[0].set_xlabel(r"$L/L_*$"); ax[0].set_ylabel(r"$\phi(L)$  [number per unit $L$]")
ax[0].legend(); ax[0].set_title("Number of galaxies")

ax[1].semilogx(L, L**2*phi, lw=2, c="C1")   # light per unit log L = L * (L phi)
ax[1].set_xlabel(r"$L/L_*$"); ax[1].set_ylabel(r"$L^2\phi(L)$  [light per $\ln L$]"); ax[1].axvline(L_star, ls=":", c="gray")
ax[1].set_title("Which galaxies hold the light")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("schechter.png", dpi=140)

Read the left panel. For $x\ll1$, $e^{-x}\approx1$, so $\phi\propto x^{-1}$: a straight line of slope −1. Faint galaxies are by far the most numerous. For $x\gtrsim1$ the exponential takes over and bright galaxies become exponentially rare. $L_*$ marks the "knee".

Light per logarithmic interval. Multiply the number per $dL$ by $L$ (light per galaxy) and by $L$ again ($dL=L\,d\ln L$):

$$\frac{dL_{\rm tot}}{d\ln L}=L^2\phi(L)\propto x\,e^{-x},\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left(xe^{-x}\right)=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=1$$

So most of the light comes from galaxies near $L_*$, even though most galaxies are faint.

Integrals. The total number $\int\phi\,dL=\phi_*\int x^{-1}e^{-x}dx$ diverges logarithmically at $x\to0$. The count depends on the faint cutoff (e.g. $\gt0.01L_*$ gives 2.2× as many as $\gt0.1L_*$). The total luminosity converges:

$$\int_0^\infty L\,\phi\,dL=\int_0^\infty (L_*x)\,\phi_*x^{-1}e^{-x}\,(L_*dx)=\phi_*L_*^2\int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx=\phi_*L_*^2$$

(Here $\phi_*=\phi(L_*)$ is per unit $L$, as in the question. For a general slope $\alpha$ the integral is $\phi_*L_*^2\,\Gamma(\alpha+2)$, finite for $\alpha\gt-2$.)

$$\boxed{\phi\propto L^{-1}\ (L\ll L_*),\ \ \propto e^{-L/L_*}\ (L\gg L_*);\ \text{light dominated by }L\approx L_*}$$

$L_*\approx2\times10^{10}L_\odot$ for real galaxies, roughly a Milky-Way-like galaxy. That's why "typical" in a light-weighted sense means MW-sized, while "typical" by number means a dwarf.

HW5 · Question 4

The case for dark matter

What evidence (or problems) have we seen that lead to the need for dark matter?

Evidence on every scale, from galaxies to the CMB

Findindependent lines of evidenceGiven—

The common thread: gravity weighs more mass than light shows, consistently and independently, on every scale.

1. Flat rotation curves of spirals. If the mass were where the light is, $v\propto r^{-1/2}$ beyond the disk. Observed $v$ stays flat out to tens of kpc. From (F.12), with $v=220$ km/s at $r=50$ kpc:

$$M(\lt50\,\mathrm{kpc})=\frac{(2.2\times10^7)^2(50\times10^3\times3.1\times10^{18})}{6.7\times10^{-8}}=1.1\times10^{45}\ \mathrm g=5.6\times10^{11}M_\odot$$

The stars and gas of the Milky Way total only $\sim6\times10^{10}M_\odot$, so about 90% of the mass is dark. Since $M(r)\propto r$, the halo has $\rho\propto r^{-2}$.

2. Galaxy clusters: dynamics. Galaxy velocity dispersions ($\sim1000$ km/s) plus the virial theorem give masses with $M/L\sim300$ (Zwicky, 1933), far above stellar $M/L\sim$ a few.

3. Galaxy clusters: hot X-ray gas. The intracluster gas at $T\sim10^7$–$10^8$ K would escape unless the gravitational well is far deeper than the gas plus galaxies can provide (hydrostatic equilibrium).

4. Gravitational lensing. Strong arcs and weak-lensing shear measure total mass directly, with no assumption about dynamics. They agree with (2) and (3). In the Bullet Cluster the lensing mass is offset from the X-ray gas, which holds most of the baryons. The mass went where the collisionless component went.

5. Cosmology. Big Bang nucleosynthesis fixes baryons at $\Omega_b\approx0.05$, but the CMB anisotropies and the growth of large-scale structure require $\Omega_m\approx0.3$. Baryons coupled to photons could not have grown today's structure from the tiny $10^{-5}$ CMB fluctuations without pre-existing dark-matter potential wells.

The problems. No particle has been detected directly, and its nature is unknown. Small-scale tensions remain (cusp vs core, missing satellites). Rotation curves alone can be fit by modified gravity (MOND, HW4 Q3), but MOND struggles with clusters, the Bullet Cluster and the CMB.

$$\boxed{\text{rotation curves, cluster dynamics + X-ray gas, lensing (Bullet), CMB + structure vs BBN}\ \Rightarrow\ \Omega_{\rm DM}\approx5\,\Omega_b}$$

The power of the case is independence. Dynamics, hot gas, lensing and the CMB use completely different physics, yet all require roughly the same extra mass.

HW5 · Question 5

Olbers' paradox

Using Olbers' paradox, convince yourself that the universe is finite in time or in space (or both).

Why the night sky is dark

Findthe contradiction, and which assumption must goGivenassume infinite, eternal, static, uniform universe of stars

Assume the universe is infinite in space, infinitely old, static, and uniformly filled with stars. Compute the sky brightness and compare with the dark night sky.

Shell argument. A shell at distance $r$, thickness $dr$, holds $n\cdot4\pi r^2dr$ stars. Each contributes flux $L/4\pi r^2$ (F.10):

$$df=n\,4\pi r^2dr\cdot\frac{L}{4\pi r^2}=nL\,dr$$

The $r^2$ cancels: every shell contributes equally. Fainter stars are exactly compensated by more of them.

Sum the shells: $f=nL\int_0^\infty dr\to\infty$. Including that nearer stars block farther ones fixes the divergence but not the paradox: every line of sight ends on a stellar surface, so the whole sky would shine with the surface brightness of a star, like standing on the Sun.

How far does a line of sight go before hitting a star? Mean free path (F.13), with stars as the Sun: $n_*\approx0.01\ \mathrm{Mpc^{-3}}\times10^{11}=10^9\ \mathrm{Mpc^{-3}}=3.4\times10^{-65}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $\sigma=\pi R_\odot^2=1.5\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$:

$$\ell=\frac{1}{n_*\sigma}=1.9\times10^{42}\ \mathrm{cm}\approx2\times10^{24}\ \text{light years}$$

Now use a finite age $t_0\approx1.4\times10^{10}$ yr. We only receive light from within $\sim ct_0$, so the covered fraction of the sky is

$$\frac{ct_0}{\ell}\approx\frac{1.4\times10^{10}}{2\times10^{24}}\approx7\times10^{-15}$$

The sky is dark. Equivalently, stars only shine for $\sim10^{10}$ yr, nowhere near enough time or energy to fill space with radiation at stellar-surface temperature.

$$\boxed{\text{dark sky}\Rightarrow\text{not (infinite + eternal + static)}:\ \text{finite age (we see out to }ct_0\ll\ell)\ \text{and/or finite extent}}$$

Cosmological expansion also helps (redshift dims distant light), but it only reduces the brightness by a factor of order unity. The decisive factor is the finite age. The modern answer: the universe has a beginning, and the only "glowing wall" we see is the CMB, redshifted from 3000 K to 2.7 K.

HW5 · Question 6

Testing a new standard candle

You suspect a previously unknown relation for a type of variable star with no period–luminosity relation (unlike Cepheids): that the pulsation depth (how much the flux varies) is related to its luminosity. How would you check this, and, if true, how would you use it to build a distance ladder to more distant galaxies?

(a) Testing the amplitude–luminosity relation

Finda test that isolates $L$ vs amplitudeGivenlight curves; distances unknown in general

The obstacle is that $L$ requires a distance. Remove it by using many stars at the same distance: then differences in apparent magnitude are differences in luminosity.

Pick a common-distance sample. Find many stars of this type in one globular/open cluster, or in the LMC/SMC. Then $m_i-M_i=\mu$ is the same for all, so $m_i=M_i+\text{const}$.

Measure two numbers per star from well-sampled light curves: mean magnitude $\langle m\rangle$ and pulsation amplitude $A=\Delta m$ (equivalently fractional depth $\Delta f/f$). Note that $A$ is a flux ratio: it is independent of distance and, to first order, of dust extinction. That is what makes it a usable candle indicator, like a period.

Plot $\langle m\rangle$ vs $A$ and test statistically. Fit a line, compute the correlation coefficient and its significance. Compare the residual scatter with the photometric errors: a usable candle needs small intrinsic scatter.

Check that it is universal and not an artefact. Repeat in several clusters/galaxies with different metallicity and age, and check the slope agrees. Watch for selection effects (faint, low-amplitude stars are missed: Malmquist bias), blending in crowded fields, and confounders (does $A$ really track $L$, or does it track temperature or period?).

$$\boxed{\text{same-distance sample}\Rightarrow\langle m\rangle\text{ vs }A\text{ fit, significance, small scatter, universal across environments}}$$

Cepheids were discovered exactly this way: Leavitt plotted apparent magnitude vs period for stars all in the SMC, a common-distance sample.

(b) Using it as a rung on the distance ladder

Findthe calibration and extrapolation chainGivenrelation $M=M_0+sA$ established in (a)

A slope alone gives only relative distances. Fix the zero point $M_0$ with a rung we already trust, then apply the relation far away.

Absolute calibration. Get the zero point from geometric distances: parallaxes (Gaia) of nearby members of the class, or the cluster/LMC distance known from Cepheids or eclipsing binaries.

Apply to a distant galaxy. Find these variables, measure the light curve → $A$ → $M=M_0+sA$. With the mean $m$ (corrected for extinction $A_\lambda$ estimated from colours), (F.10) gives

$$m-M-A_\lambda=5\log_{10}\frac{d}{10\ \mathrm{pc}}\ \Rightarrow\ d=10^{(m-M-A_\lambda+5)/5}\ \mathrm{pc}$$

Average many stars. Each star has scatter $\sigma_M$; $N$ stars reduce the distance error to $\approx0.46\,\sigma_M/\sqrt N$ in $d/d$.

Extend the ladder. Use galaxies whose distances this relation measures to calibrate brighter candles that reach farther (Type Ia supernovae, Tully–Fisher). Those reach the Hubble flow, where (F.11) takes over. Cross-check against Cepheid distances to the same galaxies.

$$\boxed{\text{parallax/cluster}\to M_0;\ \ A\to M\to d=10^{(m-M-A_\lambda+5)/5}\,\mathrm{pc};\ \ \text{calibrate SNe Ia}\to\text{Hubble flow}}$$

How far it reaches depends on how luminous the stars are: the brightest you can still detect and resolve in crowded galaxy fields sets the limit. Errors at each rung propagate upward, so the calibration in the first rung matters most.

Recap

What these two sets test

  1. Simplify the geometry before using numbers. In lensing, $D_{ls}/(D_lD_s)$ collapses to $1/D_s$ when the lens is halfway. In general, write everything in terms of $x=D_l/D_s$ and read the limits. Trap: the angular $\theta_E$ and the physical $R_E$ behave oppositely as the lens approaches us.
  2. Dimensionless variables expose the physics: $u=\beta/\theta_E$ (images at $\theta_\pm=(u\pm\sqrt{u^2+4})/2$), $r/r_0$, $L/L_*$, $D/F$. Each regime is "this number ≪ 1 or ≫ 1".
  3. Optical depth = density × cross-section × path length. If $\tau\ll1$, it is the probability. Use it for lensing, occultations and Olbers (where $\tau=ct/\ell$).
  4. Rotation curves: balance $mv^2/r$ against the force law; "flat" means $r$ cancels. MOND: $v^4=GMa_0$. A $1/r$ force gives $v^2=GM/r_0$. Trap: a universal $r_0$ gives $L\propto v^2$, not Tully–Fisher; tying $r_0=\sqrt{GM/a_0}$ fixes it.
  5. Event timescale = pattern size / relative speed. For diffraction the pattern size is $F$, not the object. Trap: the KBO's own 5 km/s is negligible next to Earth's 30 km/s, except near quadrature.
  6. Standard-candle template: a distance-independent observable (period, amplitude) → $L$ via a relation calibrated on a common-distance sample → $d$ from the flux. Zero point from geometry, then hand off to the next rung.
  7. "Each shell contributes equally" arguments (Olbers: $df=nL\,dr$) diverge. The physical cut-off (finite age, blocking) is the answer.
