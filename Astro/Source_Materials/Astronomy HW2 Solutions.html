Question 1

How a 1 M☉ and a 30 M☉ star end their lives

Briefly describe the end of life of a main-sequence star of one solar mass, and of about 30 solar masses.

Two endings, set by mass

Findthe late evolution and final remnant for $M=1\,M_\odot$ and $M=30\,M_\odot$Givenmain-sequence stars; (F.4) for the timescale

Mass decides two things: how hot the core can get (so which fuels ignite), and whether the final core is light enough for degeneracy pressure to hold it up.

How long each lives. From (F.1) and (F.4), $t/t_\odot=M/L=M^{1-3.5}=M^{-2.5}$:

$$t(1\,M_\odot)\approx10^{10}\ \mathrm{yr},\qquad t(30\,M_\odot)=30^{-2.5}\times10^{10}\approx2\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{yr}$$

The heavy star burns 30× more fuel ~150 000× faster.

1 M☉, step 1: red giant. When core hydrogen runs out, the helium core contracts and heats; hydrogen keeps burning in a shell around it. The released energy puffs the envelope up to ~100 $R_\odot$: the star becomes a cool, luminous red giant.

1 M☉, step 2: helium burning. The inert core is electron-degenerate. At ~10⁸ K helium ignites explosively (the helium flash), then burns quietly to carbon and oxygen (horizontal branch).

1 M☉, step 3: no further ignition. The C/O core is too light to reach ~6×10⁸ K for carbon burning. The star climbs the asymptotic giant branch with H and He shells, pulses, and loses its envelope in strong winds.

1 M☉, end: planetary nebula + white dwarf. The ejected envelope glows as a planetary nebula, lit by the exposed hot core. That core, ~0.6 $M_\odot$ of C/O about the size of Earth, is a white dwarf held up by electron degeneracy pressure; it cools for billions of years.

30 M☉, step 1: rapid burning and mass loss. After ~2 Myr on the main sequence it becomes a blue/red supergiant. Strong radiation-driven winds strip much of its envelope (it may become a Wolf–Rayet star).

30 M☉, step 2: the onion. The massive core reaches each ignition temperature in turn: H → He → C → Ne → O → Si, each stage shorter (Si burning lasts about a day), leaving an onion of shells around an iron core.

30 M☉, step 3: iron is a dead end. Fusing iron absorbs energy instead of releasing it. When the iron core exceeds the Chandrasekhar mass (Q2), electron degeneracy fails and the core collapses in under a second.

30 M☉, end: supernova + black hole. The collapse rebounds and drives a core-collapse supernova (Type II, or Ib/c if the envelope was stripped). For a star this heavy the remnant is most likely a black hole, possibly via fallback onto a short-lived neutron star.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}1\,M_\odot&:\ \text{red giant}\to\text{He flash}\to\text{AGB}\to\text{planetary nebula}+\text{C/O white dwarf}\ (\sim0.6\,M_\odot)\\30\,M_\odot&:\ \text{supergiant}\to\text{burning up to Fe}\to\text{core collapse}\to\text{supernova}+\text{black hole}\end{aligned}}$$

The dividing line is ~8 M☉. Below it the core never ignites carbon and ends as a white dwarf under 1.44 $M_\odot$. Above it the core burns all the way to iron and collapses. The 1 M☉ star is far below; the 30 M☉ far above.

Why the heavy star has a shorter life despite more fuel: $L\propto M^{3.5}$ grows much faster than the fuel $\propto M$.

Question 2

The Chandrasekhar mass

What is the Chandrasekhar mass? Describe qualitatively how it is obtained. What is likely to happen to a stellar remnant whose mass exceeds it?

What it is, where it comes from, and what happens above it

Finddefinition, a qualitative derivation, the fate above itGiven(F.11), (F.12); a white dwarf supported by degenerate electrons

Compare how degeneracy pressure and gravity scale with radius. Once the electrons become relativistic, both scale the same way, so a single mass balances them and nothing heavier can.

Definition. The Chandrasekhar mass is the largest mass a star supported by electron degeneracy pressure (a white dwarf) can have (F.11):

$$M_\text{Ch}\approx\frac{5.83}{\mu_e^2}M_\odot=1.46\,M_\odot\quad(\mu_e=2\text{ for C/O or He})$$

Degeneracy pressure. Pauli exclusion forces electrons into momentum states up to $p_F$ (F.12). For $N_e$ electrons in a sphere of radius $R$, $n_e\propto N_e/R^3$, so

$$p_F\propto\frac{N_e^{1/3}}{R}$$

Energy per electron, two regimes.

$$\text{non-relativistic: }\ \varepsilon\sim\frac{p_F^2}{2m_e}\propto\frac{N_e^{2/3}}{R^2},\qquad\text{relativistic: }\ \varepsilon\sim p_Fc\propto\frac{N_e^{1/3}}{R}$$

Gravity per particle scales as $-GMm_p/R\propto-N/R$.

Low mass: stable. Non-relativistic kinetic energy ($\propto1/R^2$) wins at small $R$, gravity ($\propto1/R$) wins at large $R$, so the total energy has a minimum: an equilibrium radius. Adding mass makes the star smaller and its electrons faster.

High mass: the electrons turn relativistic. Now both terms go as $1/R$:

$$E_\text{per particle}\approx\frac{1}{R}\left(\underbrace{\hbar c\,N_e^{1/3}}_{\text{degeneracy}}-\underbrace{GMm_p}_{\text{gravity}}\right)$$

$R$ factors out, so its sign decides everything: positive → expand until non-relativistic again; negative → shrinking always lowers the energy, so the star collapses with no bottom.

Set the bracket to zero. With $N\sim M/m_p$ (and $N_e\sim N$ up to $\mu_e$): $\hbar c\,N^{1/3}\sim GNm_p^2$, so $N^{2/3}\sim\hbar c/(Gm_p^2)$, giving

$$M_\text{Ch}\sim m_p\left(\frac{\hbar c}{Gm_p^2}\right)^{3/2}\approx1.9\,M_\odot$$

The scale is right; the careful calculation (Lane–Emden polytrope $n=3$) gives 1.46 $M_\odot$.

Above $M_\text{Ch}$. A remnant (or iron core) heavier than this collapses. Electrons are pushed into protons (inverse β-decay), making a neutron star held up by neutron degeneracy and nuclear forces. That has its own limit (~2–3 $M_\odot$); above it nothing stops collapse and a black hole forms. A white dwarf pushed over the limit by accretion from a companion instead ignites carbon and explodes as a Type Ia supernova, leaving no remnant.

$$\boxed{M_\text{Ch}\approx1.4\,M_\odot:\ \text{max. mass held by electron degeneracy (relativistic }e^-\Rightarrow\text{both terms}\propto1/R).\ \text{Above it: collapse}\to\text{neutron star or black hole (or Ia if accreting).}}$$

The counter-intuitive part: heavier white dwarfs are smaller. Radius shrinks with mass ($R\propto M^{-1/3}$ in the non-relativistic regime) and goes to zero as $M\to M_\text{Ch}$.

Connection to Q1: this is exactly why a 1 M☉ star's 0.6 M☉ core can retire as a white dwarf, while a 30 M☉ star's iron core, once past ~1.4 M☉, must collapse.

Question 3

A 20 M☉ main-sequence star

For a 20 M☉ main-sequence star, estimate its radius, temperature and lifetime in solar units, using the main-sequence scaling relations.

Radius, temperature, lifetime

Find$R/R_\odot$, $T/T_\odot$, $t/t_\odot$Given$M=20\,M_\odot$; (F.1)–(F.4)

Each scaling is a power law in M, so every answer is $20^{\text{some power}}$. Derive the power for T and t first, then put in 20. (Same relations as HW1 Q2(c), there used in reverse.)

Radius from (F.2):

$$\frac{R}{R_\odot}=20^{0.8}=11.0$$

Luminosity from (F.1), needed for T and t:

$$\frac{L}{L_\odot}=20^{3.5}=3.58\times10^{4}$$

Temperature: solve (F.3) for T.

$$\frac{T}{T_\odot}=\left(\frac{L/L_\odot}{(R/R_\odot)^2}\right)^{1/4}$$

Insert the power laws — exponents combine:

$$\frac{T}{T_\odot}=\left(\frac{M^{3.5}}{M^{1.6}}\right)^{1/4}=M^{1.9/4}=M^{0.475}=20^{0.475}=4.15$$ $$T\approx4.15\times5772\ \mathrm{K}\approx2.4\times10^4\ \mathrm{K}$$

Lifetime from (F.4) with (F.1):

$$\frac{t}{t_\odot}=\frac{M/M_\odot}{L/L_\odot}=M^{1-3.5}=20^{-2.5}=5.6\times10^{-4}$$ $$t\approx5.6\times10^{-4}\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{yr}\approx5.6\times10^6\ \mathrm{yr}$$
$$\boxed{R\approx11\,R_\odot,\quad T\approx4.2\,T_\odot\approx2.4\times10^4\ \mathrm{K},\quad L\approx3.6\times10^4L_\odot,\quad t\approx5.6\times10^{-4}t_\odot\approx6\ \mathrm{Myr}}$$

Reality check. Real 20 M☉ stars (late O / early B) have $R\approx7$–$8\,R_\odot$, $T\approx3.3\times10^4$ K, $L\approx5\times10^4L_\odot$ and live ~8–10 Myr. The single power laws are fitted mostly to Sun-like stars and flatten at high mass ($L\propto M^{3}$ or less), so our numbers are right in order of magnitude only.

Quick exponent trick: always reduce to one power of M before inserting numbers ($T\propto M^{0.475}$, $t\propto M^{-2.5}$). It saves arithmetic and lets you check slopes.

Question 4

Masses of an eclipsing spectroscopic binary

A spectroscopic binary with near-circular orbits is also eclipsing, so the inclination is ~90° (seen edge-on). The stars' maximum radial velocities are 20 km/s and 5 km/s, and the period is 10 years. Find each star's mass.

$M_1$ and $M_2$ from $v_1$, $v_2$, $P$

Find$M_1$, $M_2$Given$v_1=20$ km/s, $v_2=5$ km/s (max radial = full orbital speed since $i=90^\circ$), $P=10$ yr $=3.156\times10^8$ s, circular orbits

The velocities give the orbit sizes, Kepler III gives the total mass, and the centre-of-mass condition splits it. Eclipses matter because they make $\sin i=1$, so the radial velocities are the true speeds.

Edge-on ⇒ no projection. The radial velocity is $v\sin i$; with $i=90^\circ$, $\sin i=1$, so the measured maxima are the orbital speeds themselves.

Orbit radii from (F.6), $r=vP/2\pi$:

$$a_1=\frac{v_1P}{2\pi},\qquad a_2=\frac{v_2P}{2\pi}\quad\Rightarrow\quad a=a_1+a_2=\frac{(v_1+v_2)P}{2\pi}$$

Total mass from (F.5). Substitute $a$:

$$M_1+M_2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{GP^2}=\frac{4\pi^2}{GP^2}\cdot\frac{(v_1+v_2)^3P^3}{8\pi^3}$$

Simplify — $4\pi^2/8\pi^3=1/2\pi$, and $P^3/P^2=P$:

$$M_1+M_2=\frac{(v_1+v_2)^3P}{2\pi G}$$

Numbers. $v_1+v_2=2.5\times10^4$ m/s:

$$M_1+M_2=\frac{(2.5\times10^4)^3\times3.156\times10^8}{2\pi\times6.674\times10^{-11}}=\frac{4.93\times10^{21}}{4.19\times10^{-10}}=1.18\times10^{31}\ \mathrm{kg}=5.91\,M_\odot$$

(Check: $a=8.39$ AU, and $a^3/P^2=591/100=5.91$ in solar units ✓.)

Split with the centre of mass (F.7). Both stars share the same period, so $v\propto a$, and

$$M_1a_1=M_2a_2\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{M_1}{M_2}=\frac{a_2}{a_1}=\frac{v_2}{v_1}=\frac{5}{20}=\frac14$$

The faster star is the lighter one.

Individual masses:

$$M_\text{fast}=\frac{v_\text{slow}}{v_1+v_2}M_\text{tot}=\frac{5}{25}\times5.91=1.18\,M_\odot,\qquad M_\text{slow}=\frac{20}{25}\times5.91=4.73\,M_\odot$$
$$\boxed{M(v=20\ \mathrm{km/s})\approx1.2\,M_\odot,\qquad M(v=5\ \mathrm{km/s})\approx4.7\,M_\odot\qquad(M_\text{tot}=5.9\,M_\odot,\ a=8.4\ \mathrm{AU})}$$

Why eclipses are gold. For a non-eclipsing system you only get $M_\text{tot}\sin^3i$, a lower limit. An eclipse forces $i\approx90^\circ$, so you get true masses, one of the few direct ways to weigh stars.

Trap: mixing up which mass goes with which velocity. The heavier star sits closer to the centre of mass and moves slower.

Question 5

Falling toward a 10 M☉ black hole

(a) Briefly describe the Schwarzschild radius and give its value. (b) An astronaut approaches a 10 M☉ black hole feet first. At what distance (in Schwarzschild radii) will he feel his body stretched with a tension equal to his Earth weight, if he has a mass of 100 kg? (c) He transmits on a radio calibrated to 120 MHz. To what frequency should a person in a spaceship at rest far from the black hole tune his receiver?

(a) The Schwarzschild radius

Findmeaning and value of $R_s$Given(F.8); $M=10\,M_\odot$ for the rest of the question

It is the size of the event horizon: the radius inside which even light cannot escape.

Meaning. For a non-rotating mass $M$, $R_s$ is the radius of the event horizon. A body compressed inside $R_s$ becomes a black hole; nothing that crosses it, not even light, can return. (Newtonian shortcut: setting the escape speed $\sqrt{2GM/r}$ equal to $c$ gives the same formula.)

Value from (F.8):

$$R_s=\frac{2GM}{c^2}=\frac{2\times6.674\times10^{-11}\times1.989\times10^{30}}{(3.00\times10^8)^2}\left(\frac{M}{M_\odot}\right)=2.95\ \mathrm{km}\times\frac{M}{M_\odot}$$ $$R_s(10\,M_\odot)=29.5\ \mathrm{km}$$
$$\boxed{R_s=\frac{2GM}{c^2}\approx3\ \mathrm{km}\,(M/M_\odot);\quad R_s(10\,M_\odot)\approx29.5\ \mathrm{km}}$$

Linear in mass: a supermassive black hole of $4\times10^6M_\odot$ (Sgr A*) has $R_s\approx0.08$ AU. This matters in (b).

(b) Where the tidal stretch equals his Earth weight

Find$r/R_s$ where the tidal stretching force $=mg$Given$M=10\,M_\odot$, $m=100$ kg, body length $\ell\approx2$ m (assumed), $g=9.81$ m/s²

Gravity pulls his feet harder than his head; the difference is the stretch. Set it equal to $mg$ and solve for $r$. The mass cancels.

Tidal difference (F.9). Differentiate $a(r)=GM/r^2$: $\;da/dr=-2GM/r^3$, so over his length $\ell$

$$\Delta a=\frac{2GM}{r^3}\,\ell\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad F_\text{tidal}=m\,\Delta a=\frac{2GMm\ell}{r^3}$$

Set equal to his weight; $m$ appears on both sides and is nonzero, so it cancels:

$$\frac{2GMm\ell}{r^3}=mg\quad\Rightarrow\quad r=\left(\frac{2GM\ell}{g}\right)^{1/3}$$

Numbers:

$$r=\left(\frac{2\times6.674\times10^{-11}\times1.989\times10^{31}\times2}{9.81}\right)^{1/3}=(5.41\times10^{20})^{1/3}=8.15\times10^6\ \mathrm{m}$$

In Schwarzschild radii:

$$\frac{r}{R_s}=\frac{8.15\times10^6}{2.95\times10^4}\approx276$$
$$\boxed{r=\left(\frac{2GM\ell}{g}\right)^{1/3}\approx8.2\times10^3\ \mathrm{km}\approx2.8\times10^{2}\,R_s\quad(\ell=2\ \mathrm{m};\ m\text{ cancels})}$$

Refinement: tension at his waist. The difference in pull is shared along the body. For a uniform body the maximum tension (at the middle) is $T=\frac{GMm\ell}{4r^3}$, 8× smaller than the simple estimate. Then $r=(GM\ell/4g)^{1/3}=4.1\times10^3$ km $\approx138\,R_s$. Both models give "a few hundred $R_s$, thousands of km away".

At the horizon of this black hole the stretch is ~2×10⁷ g: spaghettification well outside the horizon. For Sgr A* the tide at the horizon is only ~10⁻⁴ g, so you would cross it without noticing. Tides go as $M/r^3$, and with $r\propto M$ that is $\propto1/M^2$.

Surplus data: the 100 kg is not needed, because both sides of the condition are proportional to $m$.

(c) Frequency received far away

Find$f_\infty$ for a 120 MHz signal sent from $r=276\,R_s$Given(F.10); receiver at rest far away

Light climbing out of a gravity well loses energy, so it arrives at a lower frequency. Only the ratio $R_s/r$ matters, which we have from (b).

Gravitational redshift (F.10):

$$f_\infty=f_\text{em}\sqrt{1-\frac{R_s}{r}}=120\ \mathrm{MHz}\times\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{276}}$$

Numbers. $1/276=3.62\times10^{-3}$, so $\sqrt{0.99638}=0.99819$:

$$f_\infty=119.78\ \mathrm{MHz}$$

Small-shift form (binomial, since $R_s/r\ll1$): $\sqrt{1-x}\approx1-x/2$, so

$$\Delta f\approx f_\text{em}\frac{R_s}{2r}=120\times\frac{1}{552}=0.22\ \mathrm{MHz}$$
$$\boxed{f_\infty=120\sqrt{1-R_s/r}\approx119.8\ \mathrm{MHz}\quad(\text{redshifted by }\approx0.22\ \mathrm{MHz})}$$

If the astronaut is falling, add Doppler. (F.10) is for an emitter at rest at $r$. Falling from rest at infinity, he moves away from the receiver at $\beta=\sqrt{R_s/r}=0.06$, which lowers the frequency further, to ≈112.8 MHz. The question gives no speed, so the static answer is the intended one.

With the waist-tension radius (138 $R_s$): $f_\infty=119.56$ MHz. Near the horizon (1.1 R_s) $f_\infty\to0$: the signal is infinitely redshifted.

Recap

What this homework tests

  1. Mass is destiny. <8 M☉ → white dwarf after a planetary nebula; >8 M☉ → burns to iron → core collapse → neutron star or (very massive) black hole. Lifetime $\propto M^{-2.5}$.
  2. Limits come from matching scalings. Degeneracy energy goes from $1/R^2$ to $1/R$ when electrons turn relativistic; once it scales like gravity, there's one critical mass, $M_\text{Ch}\sim m_p(\hbar c/Gm_p^2)^{3/2}\approx1.4\,M_\odot$. Trap: heavier white dwarfs are smaller, not larger.
  3. Power-law bookkeeping. Reduce every main-sequence quantity to one power of M first ($T\propto M^{0.475}$, $t\propto M^{-2.5}$), then plug in.
  4. Binary masses in four lines: $a=(v_1+v_2)P/2\pi$ → $M_\text{tot}=(v_1+v_2)^3P/2\pi G$ → $M_1/M_2=v_2/v_1$. Eclipses give $\sin i=1$. Trap: the fast star is the light one.
  5. Black holes: $R_s\approx3$ km per M☉; tides $\Delta a=2GM\ell/r^3$ (mass of the victim cancels); redshift $f_\infty=f\sqrt{1-R_s/r}\approx f(1-R_s/2r)$ far out.
