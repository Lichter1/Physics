Question 1

Why Galileo needed a telescope for Jupiter's moons

Assume the eye detects a sky object if it receives ~150 visible photons/s. Using online data (solar luminosity, the Galilean moons), estimate the flux from a typical Jovian moon reaching a 4 cm telescope and a human pupil. Find the angular separation of the moons from Jupiter: can the eye separate them? So why is a telescope needed?

(a) Photon rate from a moon: eye vs 4 cm telescope

Findvisible photons/s from a Galilean moon into a 7 mm pupil and a 4 cm apertureGiven$L_\odot$, Jupiter at 5.2 AU from the Sun, ≈4.2 AU from Earth at opposition; moon radii and albedos (table); threshold 150 photons/s

Follow the light: Sun → moon (intercepted) → reflected → spread over a sphere of radius Earth–Jupiter → caught by an aperture. Do it for Ganymede, then tabulate all four.

Solar flux at Jupiter's orbit. From (F.1) with $d=5.2$ AU $=7.78\times10^{11}$ m:

$$f_\odot=\frac{L_\odot}{4\pi(5.2\,\mathrm{AU})^2}=\frac{3.83\times10^{26}}{4\pi(7.78\times10^{11})^2}=50.3\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$$

(That is the Earth value 1361 W/m² divided by $5.2^2=27$.)

Power the moon intercepts and reflects. The moon catches sunlight on its cross-section $\pi R^2$ and reflects a fraction $A$ (F.3). Ganymede: $R=2634$ km, $A\approx0.43$:

$$P_\text{refl}=0.43\times50.3\times\pi(2.634\times10^6)^2=0.43\times50.3\times2.18\times10^{13}=4.7\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{W}$$

Spread it over a sphere centred on the moon. Treat the reflected light as roughly isotropic; at Earth, $D\approx4.2$ AU $=6.28\times10^{11}$ m away (F.1):

$$f_\text{moon}=\frac{P_\text{refl}}{4\pi D^2}=\frac{4.7\times10^{14}}{4.96\times10^{24}}=9.5\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$$

Keep the visible part and convert to photons. Roughly 40% of sunlight is in 400–700 nm. A typical visible photon (550 nm) carries, from (F.2), $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda=3.6\times10^{-19}$ J, so

$$\Phi=\frac{0.4\times9.5\times10^{-11}}{3.6\times10^{-19}}=1.05\times10^{8}\ \text{photons s}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-2}$$

Multiply by each collecting area. Dark-adapted pupil $D_\text{eye}\approx7$ mm, telescope $D_\text{tel}=4$ cm:

$$N_\text{eye}=\Phi\,\pi\left(\tfrac{7\times10^{-3}}{2}\right)^2=1.05\times10^8\times3.85\times10^{-5}\approx4\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$$ $$N_\text{tel}=\Phi\,\pi\left(\tfrac{4\times10^{-2}}{2}\right)^2=1.05\times10^8\times1.26\times10^{-3}\approx1.3\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$$

The gain is just the area ratio, $(40/7)^2\approx33$.

Sanity check with magnitudes. Ganymede is about magnitude 4.6 at opposition. Vega (mag 0) delivers ~10¹⁰ visible photons s⁻¹ m⁻²; 4.6 mag fainter is a factor 10^(0.4·4.6)≈70 → 1.4×10⁸ s⁻¹ m⁻², matching our 1.05×10⁸. The naked-eye limit in a dark sky is mag ~6, so the moons are bright enough on their own.

Factor-of-two honesty. A full-phase Lambertian sphere throws more light back toward the Sun than an isotropic one, and the pupil may be 5 mm rather than 7. Each changes $N_\text{eye}$ by a factor ≲2. Nothing brings it down to 150.

(b) Angular separation from Jupiter, and can the eye resolve it?

Findmaximum angular separation of each moon; compare with the eye's resolutionGivenorbital radii $a$; Earth–Jupiter distance $D\approx6.28\times10^{11}$ m

At maximum elongation the moon's orbit radius is seen side-on, so the small-angle relation gives the separation directly.

Small angle (F.4) for Io, $a=4.22\times10^8$ m:

$$\theta=\frac{a}{D}=\frac{4.22\times10^{8}}{6.28\times10^{11}}=6.7\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad}\times\frac{206265''}{1\ \mathrm{rad}}=138''=2.3'$$

Resolution of the eye. The diffraction limit of a 7 mm pupil (F.5) is

$$\theta_\text{min}=1.22\,\frac{550\times10^{-9}}{7\times10^{-3}}=9.6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}\approx20''$$

but the retina's cone spacing limits real acuity to about $1'$. For the 4 cm telescope, (F.5) gives $3.5''$.

Compare. $\theta_\text{max}=2.3'$–$10.3'$ is larger than $1'$, so for two equally bright points the eye could separate them, at least near maximum elongation (most of the time the moon is closer to Jupiter than $\theta_\text{max}$).

$$\boxed{\theta_\text{max}\approx2.3'\ (\text{Io})\ \text{to}\ 10.3'\ (\text{Callisto})\ \gt\ \theta_\text{eye}\approx1'}$$

The catch is "equally bright". Resolution criteria assume similar sources. Jupiter reflects $0.52\cdot f_\odot\cdot\pi R_J^2\approx4\times10^{17}$ W, about 850× Ganymede (≈7 magnitudes brighter). The eye's optics scatter a little of Jupiter's light into a halo several arcminutes wide, and a point 850× fainter inside that halo is invisible.

(c) So why is a telescope needed?

Findthe reasons, weighed by the numbers aboveGiven(a) and (b)

Put (a) and (b) side by side: photons are not the bottleneck; contrast next to a very bright neighbour is.

Light-gathering helps, but only as a margin: the telescope raises the photon rate ×33, from ~10³ to ~10⁵ s⁻¹. This matters once magnification spreads Jupiter's sky background and the moon's image, and it lets you see the moons through haze or moonlight.

Magnification is the decisive part. A telescope multiplies apparent angles by $M$. At $M=20$, Io appears $2.3'\times20=46'$ from Jupiter, far outside the few-arcminute glare halo of the eye, and Jupiter's disk ($46''$ true, $15'$ apparent) becomes a resolved disk instead of a dazzling point.

Resolution also improves (3.5″ vs ~60″), so the moon image is a sharp point against a dark sky rather than a smear merging with Jupiter.

$$\boxed{\text{Main reason: contrast — moons }\sim10^3\times\text{ fainter, only }2'\text{–}10'\text{ from Jupiter's glare. The telescope separates them (}\times M\text{) and adds light (}\times33).}$$

Consistent with history. Sharp-eyed observers do occasionally report Ganymede or Callisto with the naked eye at maximum elongation, especially when Jupiter is hidden behind a rooftop. That is exactly what the numbers predict: enough photons, ruined by glare.

Question 2

A star from a phone on a telescope

A star has total (bolometric) flux $f=3.4\times10^{-4}$ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹. The ratio of its flux at 450 nm (blue) to 550 nm (green) is 0.6. Half a year later it has shifted 0.2″ relative to the background stars. (a) Find L, distance, T and R, assuming the Wien limit $h\nu\gg k_BT$. (b) Could you find T and R in the Rayleigh–Jeans limit $h\nu\ll k_BT$? Show explicitly. (c) Do the values fit main-sequence relations (e.g. relative to the Sun)?

(a) Distance, luminosity, temperature, radius

Find$d$, $L$, $T$, $R$Given$f=3.4\times10^{-4}$ erg cm⁻² s⁻¹; $F_{450}/F_{550}=0.6$; shift 0.2″ over half a year; Wien limit

Parallax gives the distance; distance + flux gives L; the colour gives T (the radius cancels in a ratio); then Stefan–Boltzmann gives R.

Parallax angle. Half a year apart, Earth is at opposite ends of its orbit, so the star moves across the full width of its parallax ellipse, $2p$:

$$2p=0.2''\ \Rightarrow\ p=0.1''$$

Distance from (F.6):

$$d=\frac{1}{0.1}=10\ \mathrm{pc}=10\times3.086\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{cm}=3.09\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm}$$

Luminosity — invert (F.1):

$$L=4\pi d^2f=4\pi(3.09\times10^{19})^2\times3.4\times10^{-4}=1.20\times10^{40}\times3.4\times10^{-4}=4.07\times10^{36}\ \mathrm{erg/s}$$ $$L=4.07\times10^{29}\ \mathrm{W}=\frac{4.07\times10^{29}}{3.83\times10^{26}}L_\odot\approx1060\,L_\odot$$

Colour → the radius and distance cancel. The observed flux at each wavelength is $F_\lambda=\pi B_\lambda(R/d)^2$ (F.14). In a ratio at the same star, $\pi(R/d)^2$ is a common nonzero factor, so it cancels:

$$\frac{F_{\lambda_1}}{F_{\lambda_2}}=\frac{B_{\lambda_1}(T)}{B_{\lambda_2}(T)},\qquad\lambda_1=450\ \mathrm{nm},\ \lambda_2=550\ \mathrm{nm}$$

Insert the Wien tail (F.10) — the prefactor $2hc^2$ also cancels:

$$\frac{F_{\lambda_1}}{F_{\lambda_2}}=\frac{\lambda_1^{-5}e^{-hc/\lambda_1k_BT}}{\lambda_2^{-5}e^{-hc/\lambda_2k_BT}}=\left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^5\exp\!\left[-\frac{hc}{k_BT}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_1}-\frac{1}{\lambda_2}\right)\right]$$

Solve for T: divide by $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^5$, take the log, and flip signs:

$$T=\frac{\dfrac{hc}{k_B}\left(\dfrac{1}{\lambda_1}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda_2}\right)}{\ln\!\left[\left(\dfrac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^5\Big/\dfrac{F_{\lambda_1}}{F_{\lambda_2}}\right]}$$

Numbers. $\frac{1}{\lambda_1}-\frac{1}{\lambda_2}=2.222\times10^6-1.818\times10^6=4.04\times10^5$ m⁻¹, so the numerator is $1.4388\times10^{-2}\times4.04\times10^5=5813$ K. Denominator: $(550/450)^5=2.727$, $2.727/0.6=4.546$, $\ln4.546=1.514$:

$$T=\frac{5813}{1.514}=3.84\times10^3\ \mathrm{K}$$

Check the Wien assumption. At the longer wavelength, $\dfrac{hc}{\lambda k_BT}=\dfrac{1.4388\times10^{-2}}{550\times10^{-9}\times3840}=6.8\gg1$ ✓. Solving the full Planck ratio numerically gives 3841 K — the approximation costs 1 K.

Radius from (F.7):

$$R=\sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}}=\sqrt{\frac{4.07\times10^{29}}{4\pi\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times3840^4}}=\sqrt{\frac{4.07\times10^{29}}{1.55\times10^{8}}}=5.1\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m}\approx74\,R_\odot$$
$$\boxed{d=10\ \mathrm{pc},\ \ L\approx4.1\times10^{36}\ \mathrm{erg/s}\approx1.1\times10^{3}L_\odot,\ \ T\approx3.8\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{K},\ \ R\approx5.1\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{cm}\approx74\,R_\odot}$$

Other readings. If the 0.2″ is taken as $p$ itself: $d=5$ pc, $L=266\,L_\odot$, $R=37\,R_\odot$. If the ratio is per unit frequency ($F_\nu$, where the Wien prefactor is $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^3$): $T=5220$ K, $R=40\,R_\odot$. Every variant is a giant.

Why T needs only the colour. A ratio at two wavelengths of the same star removes everything geometric ($R$, $d$). That is the whole point of colour temperatures, and the reason (b) fails.

(b) The same measurement in the Rayleigh–Jeans limit

Findwhether T and R follow if $h\nu\ll k_BT$Giventhe same colour ratio and bolometric flux

Repeat the colour calculation with (F.11) and watch T cancel.

Insert the RJ form into the ratio of step (a):

$$\frac{F_{\lambda_1}}{F_{\lambda_2}}=\frac{2ck_BT/\lambda_1^4}{2ck_BT/\lambda_2^4}$$

Cancel the common factor $2ck_BT$ (nonzero):

$$\frac{F_{\lambda_1}}{F_{\lambda_2}}=\left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^4=\left(\frac{550}{450}\right)^4=2.23\quad\text{for every }T$$

The colour carries no information about $T$: every hot body has the same slope $\propto\lambda^{-4}$.

Absolute flux doesn't save it. One monochromatic flux gives $F_\lambda=\pi\left(\frac{R}{d}\right)^2\frac{2ck_BT}{\lambda^4}$, i.e. only the product $R^2T$. The bolometric flux gives $L=4\pi R^2\sigma T^4$, i.e. $R^2T^4$. With only the colour and $f$ (what we have), the colour is useless and $L$ is one equation in two unknowns.

$$\boxed{\text{No: in RJ, }F_{\lambda_1}/F_{\lambda_2}=(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^4\text{ is independent of }T,\text{ so }T\text{ and }R\text{ cannot be separated.}}$$

The data rules RJ out anyway. RJ predicts a ratio of 2.23 (or $(550/450)^2=1.49$ per frequency); the measured 0.6 means the spectrum is falling toward the blue, i.e. we are on the Wien side of the peak.

A loophole worth knowing. If you had a calibrated monochromatic flux ($\propto R^2T$) and the bolometric flux ($\propto R^2T^4$), dividing them gives $T^3$, so T and R would follow. The colour alone never can.

(c) Is this a main-sequence star?

Findcompare $L$, $T$, $R$ with main-sequence scaling relative to the SunGiven$L\approx1060\,L_\odot$, $T\approx3840$ K, $R\approx74\,R_\odot$; (F.13); $T_\odot=5772$ K

Ask what a main-sequence star of the same luminosity would look like, and compare.

Mass from luminosity (F.13): $M=1060^{1/3.5}\,M_\odot=7.3\,M_\odot$.

Its radius and temperature: $R=7.3^{0.8}R_\odot=4.9\,R_\odot$, and from (F.7) relative to the Sun

$$T=T_\odot\left(\frac{L/L_\odot}{(R/R_\odot)^2}\right)^{1/4}=5772\left(\frac{1060}{4.9^2}\right)^{1/4}\approx1.5\times10^4\ \mathrm{K}$$

Compare. A main-sequence star this luminous is a hot B star of ~5 $R_\odot$. Ours is 4× cooler and 15× larger. Conversely, a main-sequence star at 3840 K is an M dwarf with $L\approx0.05\,L_\odot$ and $R\approx0.5\,R_\odot$.

$$\boxed{\text{Not main sequence: cool }(3.8\times10^3\ \mathrm{K})\text{ yet }10^3L_\odot\Rightarrow R\approx74R_\odot\text{, a red giant (upper right of the HR diagram).}}$$

The one-line test. On the main sequence, more luminous means hotter. "Bright and red" can only be done with a large surface area, which is the definition of a giant.

Question 3

Blackbody spectra in code

(a) Write a program that plots a blackbody spectrum for a given T, versus frequency and versus wavelength. (b) Do the maxima satisfy $\lambda_\text{max}=c/\nu_\text{max}$? Explain. (c) Write a program that finds T from a colour (flux ratio at two wavelengths). (d) How would you change it for many colours with measurement errors? (e) A galaxy has 10¹⁰ stars with temperatures uniformly random in 2600–35000 K. Sum all their spectra per wavelength and plot.

(a) Planck spectrum vs ν and vs λ

Finda program plotting $B_\nu(\nu)$ and $B_\lambda(\lambda)$Given(F.8), (F.9)

Code the two Planck functions directly; first check they really are the same physics by converting one into the other.

Convert $B_\nu$ to $B_\lambda$ with (F.12). With $\nu=c/\lambda$, $|d\nu/d\lambda|=c/\lambda^2$:

$$B_\lambda=B_\nu\left|\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}\right|=\frac{2h(c/\lambda)^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1}\cdot\frac{c}{\lambda^2}=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1}\ \checkmark$$

Code it. Use np.expm1 for $e^x-1$ (accurate at small $x$), log-spaced grids, and log–log axes since the spectrum spans many decades. The full script for every part of Q3 is below; plot_blackbody is part (a).

blackbody_hw1.py
"""Astronomy 1 - HW1 Q3: blackbody spectra, colour -> temperature, galaxy sum."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize_scalar
from scipy.integrate import quad

h, c, k = 6.62607e-34, 2.99792458e8, 1.380649e-23

def B_nu(nu, T):    # W m^-2 Hz^-1 sr^-1
    return 2*h*nu**3/c**2 / np.expm1(h*nu/(k*T))

def B_lam(lam, T):  # W m^-2 m^-1 sr^-1
    return 2*h*c**2/lam**5 / np.expm1(h*c/(lam*k*T))

# (a) spectrum of one temperature, in frequency and in wavelength
def plot_blackbody(T, ax_nu, ax_lam):
    nu = np.logspace(12, 16.5, 2000); lam = c/nu
    ax_nu.loglog(nu, B_nu(nu, T), label=f"T={T:.0f} K")
    ax_lam.loglog(lam*1e9, B_lam(lam, T), label=f"T={T:.0f} K")
    nu_max, lam_max = nu[np.argmax(B_nu(nu, T))], lam[np.argmax(B_lam(lam, T))]
    print(f"T={T:.0f} K: lam_max={lam_max*1e9:.0f} nm, c/nu_max={c/nu_max*1e9:.0f} nm")   # (b)

# (c) colour (flux ratio at two wavelengths) -> temperature
def T_from_colour(ratio, lam1, lam2, Tlo=500, Thi=1e6):
    return brentq(lambda T: B_lam(lam1, T)/B_lam(lam2, T) - ratio, Tlo, Thi)

# (d) several colours with errors -> chi^2 fit of a single T
def T_from_colours(ratios, sigmas, pairs):
    chi2 = lambda T: sum(((r - B_lam(a, T)/B_lam(b, T))/s)**2
                         for r, s, (a, b) in zip(ratios, sigmas, pairs))
    return minimize_scalar(chi2, bounds=(500, 1e5), method="bounded").x

# (e) galaxy: N stars, T uniform in [Tmin, Tmax], all with the same radius
def galaxy_spectrum(lam, N=1e10, Tmin=2600, Tmax=35000, R=6.957e8, n_sample=20000, seed=1):
    Ts = np.random.default_rng(seed).uniform(Tmin, Tmax, n_sample)
    per_star = np.array([B_lam(l, Ts).mean() for l in lam])          # Monte-Carlo average
    exact = np.array([quad(lambda T: B_lam(l, T), Tmin, Tmax)[0] for l in lam])/(Tmax - Tmin)
    L = N * 4*np.pi**2*R**2                                           # L_lam = N * 4 pi R^2 * pi B_lam
    return L*per_star, L*exact

if __name__ == "__main__":
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4.3))
    for T in (3000, 5800, 15000):
        plot_blackbody(T, ax[0], ax[1])
    ax[0].set(xlabel="ν [Hz]", ylabel="B_ν", title="(a) Planck vs frequency", ylim=(1e-12, 1e-4)); ax[0].legend()
    ax[1].set(xlabel="λ [nm]", ylabel="B_λ", title="(a) Planck vs wavelength", ylim=(1e3, 1e16)); ax[1].legend()
    print("(c) ratio 0.6 at 450/550 nm ->", round(T_from_colour(0.6, 450e-9, 550e-9)), "K")
    pairs = [(400e-9, 550e-9), (450e-9, 550e-9), (550e-9, 700e-9)]
    true = [B_lam(a, 6000)/B_lam(b, 6000) for a, b in pairs]
    noisy = np.array(true)*(1 + 0.02*np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(3))
    print("(d) fit of 3 noisy colours (true 6000 K) ->", round(T_from_colours(noisy, 0.02*np.array(true), pairs)), "K")
    lam = np.logspace(np.log10(50e-9), np.log10(5e-6), 300)
    mc, ex = galaxy_spectrum(lam)
    ax[2].loglog(lam*1e9, ex, label="sum of 10¹⁰ stars (exact integral)")
    ax[2].loglog(lam*1e9, mc, "--", label="Monte-Carlo, 2×10⁴ samples ×5×10⁵")
    for T in (2600, 35000):
        ax[2].loglog(lam*1e9, 1e10*4*np.pi**2*6.957e8**2*B_lam(lam, T), ":", label=f"10¹⁰ stars all at {T} K")
    ax[2].set(xlabel="λ [nm]", ylabel="L_λ [W/m]", title="(e) galaxy: summed spectrum", ylim=(ex.max()*1e-6, ex.max()*30)); ax[2].legend(fontsize=7)
    plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("hw1_q3.png", dpi=140)
$$\boxed{B_\nu=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1},\qquad B_\lambda=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1}\quad\text{(plotted by the script)}}$$

Log axes reveal the limits. On the left the low-frequency side is a straight line of slope 2 ($B_\nu\propto\nu^2$, RJ); on the right the long-wavelength side has slope −4. The steep cliff is the Wien tail, where (F.10) applies.

(b) Is $\lambda_\text{max}=c/\nu_\text{max}$?

Findthe two peaks and their relationGiven(F.8), (F.9)

Find each maximum with a dimensionless variable; the two equations differ by the power of $x$, so the peaks differ.

Peak of $B_\nu$. Let $x=h\nu/k_BT$; then $B_\nu\propto x^3/(e^x-1)$. Set the derivative to zero:

$$\frac{3x^2(e^x-1)-x^3e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}=0\ \Rightarrow\ 3(1-e^{-x})=x\ \Rightarrow\ x_\nu=2.821$$

Peak of $B_\lambda$. Let $x=hc/\lambda k_BT$; then $B_\lambda\propto x^5/(e^x-1)$, and the same steps give

$$5(1-e^{-x})=x\ \Rightarrow\ x_\lambda=4.965$$

Translate both into wavelengths.

$$\lambda_\text{max}=\frac{hc}{4.965\,k_BT}=\frac{2.898\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}}{T},\qquad\frac{c}{\nu_\text{max}}=\frac{hc}{2.821\,k_BT}=\frac{5.10\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}}{T}$$ $$\frac{c/\nu_\text{max}}{\lambda_\text{max}}=\frac{4.965}{2.821}=1.76$$

For the Sun (5800 K): $\lambda_\text{max}=500$ nm but $c/\nu_\text{max}=880$ nm.

$$\boxed{\lambda_\text{max}\neq c/\nu_\text{max};\quad c/\nu_\text{max}\approx1.76\,\lambda_\text{max}\text{ at every }T}$$

Why. $B_\nu$ and $B_\lambda$ are densities: energy per unit ν versus per unit λ. Converting needs the Jacobian $c/\lambda^2$ (F.12), which multiplies more weight onto short wavelengths, so the λ-curve peaks bluer. A fixed 1 nm bin at 300 nm covers far more Hz than a 1 nm bin at 1000 nm. A maximum of a density is not invariant under a change of variable, while the total area (the energy) is.

(c) Program: colour → temperature

FindT from $r=F_{\lambda_1}/F_{\lambda_2}$Giventhe exact Planck ratio (no limit assumed)

Exactly as in Q2(a), the ratio depends on T only; find the T where the model ratio equals the measured one with a bracketed root-finder.

Model. From (F.8), radius and distance cancel as in Q2(a):

$$r(T)=\frac{B_\lambda(\lambda_1,T)}{B_\lambda(\lambda_2,T)}$$

Monotonic, so the root is unique. For $\lambda_1\lt\lambda_2$, $r(T)$ rises from 0 (cold) to the RJ ceiling $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^4$ (hot). Any $r$ below that ceiling has exactly one $T$; brentq on $r(T)-r_\text{obs}$ over [500, 10⁶] K finds it (function T_from_colour).

Test on Q2's star: $r=0.6$ at 450/550 nm → $T=3841$ K, matching the Wien-limit hand result 3840 K.

$$\boxed{T:\ \frac{B_\lambda(\lambda_1,T)}{B_\lambda(\lambda_2,T)}=r_\text{obs}\ \ (\text{root-find; }0.6\text{ at }450/550\text{ nm}\to3841\ \mathrm{K})}$$

Built-in error check. If $r_\text{obs}\ge(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^4$ there is no root: the object is not a blackbody (or the measurement is off). The code should report that instead of returning nonsense.

(d) Many colours with errors

Findhow to estimate one T from several colours $r_i\pm\sigma_i$Giventhe model from (c)

With more data than unknowns, stop solving equations and start fitting: minimise a weighted misfit.

Define χ². Each colour pulls with weight $1/\sigma_i^2$:

$$\chi^2(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{r_i-r_i^\text{model}(T)}{\sigma_i}\right)^2$$

Minimise over T (function T_from_colours, bounded 1-D minimiser). Test: three colours of a 6000 K body with 2% noise → 6018 K.

Uncertainty. The 1σ range of T is where $\chi^2$ rises by 1 above its minimum: $\chi^2(T)=\chi^2_\text{min}+1$.

Goodness of fit. $\chi^2_\text{min}/(N-1)\approx1$ means a single blackbody describes the data; $\gg1$ means it does not (extinction, lines, two stars).

Better variant: fit the fluxes themselves. Colours that share a band are correlated. Instead fit $F_i^\text{model}=\Omega\,\pi B_\lambda(\lambda_i,T)$ with two parameters $T$ and $\Omega=(R/d)^2$. Because the model is linear in Ω, for each trial T the best Ω has a closed form, so it is still a 1-D search over T, and the fitted Ω gives $R/d$ for free.

$$\boxed{\hat T=\arg\min_T\sum_i\frac{(r_i-r_i^\text{model}(T))^2}{\sigma_i^2},\quad\delta T:\ \Delta\chi^2=1}$$

(e) A galaxy of 10¹⁰ stars

Find$L_\lambda^\text{gal}$ summed over all stars; plot itGiven$N=10^{10}$, $T\sim U(2600,35000)$ K; radii not given → assume all $R=R_\odot$

Ten billion function calls is pointless: a sum over many random draws is N times the average, and for a uniform distribution the average is an integral.

One star's spectral luminosity (surface flux $\pi B_\lambda$ times area):

$$L_\lambda^{(i)}=4\pi R^2\cdot\pi B_\lambda(\lambda,T_i)$$

Sum → N × mean (law of large numbers; with $10^{10}$ draws the scatter is negligible):

$$L_\lambda^\text{gal}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}L_\lambda^{(i)}\approx N\cdot4\pi^2R^2\,\langle B_\lambda(\lambda,T)\rangle$$

Uniform T → integral. The density of T is $1/(T_\text{max}-T_\text{min})$:

$$\langle B_\lambda\rangle=\frac{1}{T_\text{max}-T_\text{min}}\int_{T_\text{min}}^{T_\text{max}}B_\lambda(\lambda,T)\,dT$$

The script computes it both ways (quad, and a 2×10⁴-star Monte-Carlo scaled up); they agree to 0.3%.

Result. The summed spectrum peaks near 97 nm (far UV), set by the hottest stars, but it is broader than any single blackbody: on the red side it is a sum of RJ tails $\propto\langle T\rangle/\lambda^4$.

$$\boxed{L_\lambda^\text{gal}=\frac{4\pi^2R^2N}{T_\text{max}-T_\text{min}}\int_{T_\text{min}}^{T_\text{max}}B_\lambda(\lambda,T)\,dT,\quad\text{peak}\approx97\ \mathrm{nm}}$$

Who makes the light? With equal radii each star contributes $\propto T^4$, so stars above 10 000 K emit 99.8% of the total even though they are 77% of the population. Real galaxies are the opposite in numbers (most stars are cool dwarfs), which is why their optical spectra look much redder than this toy model.

Question 4

Distance from a shrinking cluster

An astronomer measures, by Doppler shift, the recession velocity $v$ of a star cluster and its angular size $\theta$. A decade later its angular size has decreased by $\Delta\theta$. Can the distance be found from this? If so, what is it?

Distance from $v$, $\theta$, $\Delta\theta$, $\Delta t$

Find$d$Given$v$ (radial), $\theta$, $\Delta\theta$ after $\Delta t=10$ yr; physical size $D$ unknown but constant

The unknown physical size cancels in the fractional change of θ, which equals the fractional change of distance — and we know how far the cluster moved, $v\Delta t$.

Small angle (F.4) at both epochs, with the cluster $v\Delta t$ farther away the second time:

$$\theta=\frac{D}{d},\qquad\theta-\Delta\theta=\frac{D}{d+v\Delta t}$$

Divide the two equations — $D$ is a common nonzero factor and cancels:

$$\frac{\theta-\Delta\theta}{\theta}=\frac{d}{d+v\Delta t}$$

Solve for d. Cross-multiply: $(\theta-\Delta\theta)(d+v\Delta t)=\theta d$, so $-\Delta\theta\,d+(\theta-\Delta\theta)v\Delta t=0$:

$$d=v\Delta t\,\frac{\theta-\Delta\theta}{\Delta\theta}$$

Small change. For $\Delta\theta\ll\theta$ this is the differential form of $\theta\propto1/d$, i.e. $\Delta\theta/\theta=\Delta d/d$:

$$d\approx v\Delta t\,\frac{\theta}{\Delta\theta}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Yes: }d=v\Delta t\,\frac{\theta-\Delta\theta}{\Delta\theta}\approx\frac{v\,\Delta t\,\theta}{\Delta\theta}}$$

Why it works. The Doppler shift gives a physical speed, the angle change gives a fractional change in distance; together they fix the absolute scale — the same idea as "expansion parallax" for nebulae.

Assumptions. The cluster's size is constant (bound, not expanding); $v$ is the radial velocity, which is what Doppler measures and what changes $d$; $v\ll c$; small angles. Scale: 100 km/s for 10 yr is 1.0×10⁻³ pc; at 1 kpc, $\Delta\theta/\theta\approx10^{-6}$, i.e. 0.6 mas for a 10′ cluster — Gaia-class astrometry.

Recap

What this homework tests

  1. Photon budget chain. Source power → intercepted fraction ($\pi R^2/4\pi d^2$) → reflected ($A$) → diluted ($1/4\pi D^2$) → band fraction → $/E_\gamma$ → × aperture area. Trap: concluding "too faint" without checking; here the photons were fine and contrast was the problem.
  2. Ratios kill geometry. A flux ratio at two wavelengths cancels $R$ and $d$, leaving T. Then $d$ (parallax) + $f$ → $L$, and $L$ + $T$ → $R$. Trap: a half-year shift is $2p$, not $p$.
  3. Know which limit you're in. Wien: colour ∝ exponential in $1/T$ → T measurable. RJ: colour fixed at $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^4$ → T unmeasurable. Check $hc/\lambda k_BT$ against 1, and check whether the measured ratio is even possible.
  4. Densities change under a change of variable. $B_\nu$ and $B_\lambda$ peak at different places ($c/\nu_\text{max}=1.76\lambda_\text{max}$) because of the Jacobian $c/\lambda^2$; totals agree, maxima don't.
  5. Fractional changes give absolute scales. $\theta\propto1/d$ ⇒ $\Delta\theta/\theta=\Delta d/d$; a known physical $\Delta d=v\Delta t$ then gives $d$. Same move: N random stars → N × an integral average.
