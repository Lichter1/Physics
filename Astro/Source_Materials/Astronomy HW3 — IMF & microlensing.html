Question 1

A Salpeter population of main-sequence blackbodies

In HW1 you computed the spectrum of a star collection with a uniform mass distribution. Upgrade the program using the initial mass function. (a) Adopt the Salpeter IMF and compute the spectrum of a collection of blackbodies born with this distribution, right after formation (use main-sequence relations). (b) Repeat 10⁹ years later, keeping only stars still on the main sequence (ignore supernovae, giants, white dwarfs…). (c) Using the IMF: which contributes more to the total mass — stars up to 8 M☉ (alternatively up to 1 M☉; try both) or more massive stars? (d) Use the mass–luminosity relation to derive the luminosity function from the IMF and plot it. Who contributes more light — low- or high-mass stars? (e) What photon wavelength ionizes hydrogen? Which stars dominate hydrogen ionization in star-forming regions?

Common step — every star's temperature from its mass

Find$T(M)$, so each star becomes a blackbody fixed by one numberGiven(F.2), (F.3), (F.4)

A blackbody is fixed by $R$ and $T$. We have $L(M)$ and $R(M)$; Stefan–Boltzmann turns those into $T(M)$, so the whole population becomes a one-parameter family.

Write (F.4) once for the star and once for the Sun, and divide — the constants $4\pi\sigma$ cancel:

$$\frac{L}{L_\odot}=\left(\frac{R}{R_\odot}\right)^2\left(\frac{T}{T_\odot}\right)^4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=T_\odot\left(\frac{L/L_\odot}{(R/R_\odot)^2}\right)^{1/4}$$

Substitute the power laws (F.2), (F.3) (masses in $M_\odot$):

$$T=T_\odot\left(\frac{M^{3.5}}{M^{1.6}}\right)^{1/4}=T_\odot\,M^{1.9/4}=T_\odot\,M^{0.475}$$

Evaluate at the IMF limits with $T_\odot=5772$ K:

$M\ [M_\odot]$0.112.5110100
$T$ [K]1 9305 7728 94017 20051 400
$$\boxed{T(M)=5772\ \mathrm{K}\,(M/M_\odot)^{0.475},\qquad L_\lambda(M)=4\pi R^2(M)\,\pi B_\lambda\!\big(T(M)\big)}$$

Sanity: 100 M☉ at ~50 000 K is an early O star — right. The 0.1 M☉ value (~1 900 K) is too cold (real late-M dwarfs are ~2 800 K) because one power law can't cover three decades of mass. It doesn't matter here: those stars carry almost no light (part d).

(a) Spectrum of the population at birth

Find$L_\lambda^{\rm pop}(\lambda)$ at $t=0$GivenSalpeter IMF (F.1) from 0.1 to 100 M☉; each star a blackbody (F.5) with $R,T$ from the common step

The population spectrum is just a weighted sum of blackbodies: "how many stars of mass M" × "what one such star emits", added over all masses. The IMF supplies the first factor, the common step the second.

Count stars in a mass bin. By definition of the IMF (F.1), the number with mass in $[M,M+dM]$ is $dN=\xi(M)\,dM$.

Add their spectra. Each emits $L_\lambda(M)$ from (F.5), so

$$L_\lambda^{\rm pop}(\lambda)=\int_{0.1}^{100}\xi(M)\;4\pi R(M)^2\;\pi B_\lambda\big(T(M)\big)\,dM$$

Drop overall constants (we only want the shape, and the IMF normalisation is arbitrary): with $\xi\propto M^{-2.35}$ and $R^2\propto M^{1.6}$,

$$L_\lambda^{\rm pop}\propto\int_{0.1}^{100}M^{-2.35}\,M^{1.6}\,B_\lambda\!\big(T_\odot M^{0.475}\big)\,dM=\int M^{-0.75}\,B_\lambda\big(T(M)\big)\,dM$$

Do the integral numerically. Use a logarithmic mass grid (600 points) — equal steps in $\log M$ resolve both the many faint stars and the few bright ones — and a log wavelength grid from 10 nm to 10 μm; trapezoid rule over $M$ for each $\lambda$. That is population_spectrum() in the program below.

Read off the result. The summed spectrum peaks at $\lambda_{\rm peak}\approx67$ nm. By (F.7) that corresponds to

$$T_{\rm eff}=\frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{67\times10^{-9}}\approx4.3\times10^4\ \mathrm{K}$$

— close to the hottest stars in the population, even though they are a tiny fraction by number.

$$\boxed{L_\lambda^{\rm pop}\propto\int_{0.1}^{100}M^{-0.75}B_\lambda(T_\odot M^{0.475})\,dM,\quad \text{peaks in the far UV at }\lambda\approx 67\ \mathrm{nm}}$$
imf_spectrum.py — the Q1 program (parts a–d)
"""HW3 Q1 - spectrum and luminosity function of a Salpeter population of main-sequence blackbodies."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

h, c, k = 6.626e-34, 2.998e8, 1.381e-23
T_sun = 5772.0                       # K
ALPHA = 2.35                         # Salpeter: dN/dM ∝ M^-2.35
A_L, A_R = 3.5, 0.8                  # main sequence: L ∝ M^3.5, R ∝ M^0.8 (solar units)
M_LO, M_HI = 0.1, 100.0              # IMF mass range [M_sun]

def T_eff(M):                        # Stefan-Boltzmann: L = 4πR²σT⁴  ->  T = T_sun (L/R²)^(1/4)
    return T_sun * (M**A_L / M**(2*A_R))**0.25

def t_ms(M):                         # main-sequence lifetime [yr]
    return 1e10 * M**-2.5

def planck(lam, T):                  # B_λ(T) [W m^-3 sr^-1]
    x = np.minimum(h*c/(lam*k*T), 700)
    return 2*h*c**2 / lam**5 / np.expm1(x)

def population_spectrum(lam, age_yr=0.0, n_mass=600):
    """L_λ of the population: Σ over stars of 4πR² · πB_λ(T), weighted by the IMF."""
    M_max = M_HI if age_yr == 0 else min(M_HI, (1e10/age_yr)**(1/2.5))   # turnoff mass
    M = np.logspace(np.log10(M_LO), np.log10(M_max), n_mass)
    w = M**-ALPHA * (M**A_R)**2                     # IMF × R²  (constants dropped)
    B = planck(lam[:, None], T_eff(M)[None, :])     # (n_lam, n_mass)
    return np.trapezoid(w * B, M, axis=1), M_max

lam = np.logspace(-8, -5, 1200)                     # 10 nm - 10 μm
S0, _   = population_spectrum(lam, 0)
S1, Mto = population_spectrum(lam, 1e9)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.loglog(lam*1e9, S0, label='t = 0 (ZAMS)')
ax.loglog(lam*1e9, S1, label=f't = 10⁹ yr (M ≤ {Mto:.2f} M☉)')
ax.axvline(91.2, ls=':', c='gray'); ax.text(95, S0.max()*1e-6, '91.2 nm', color='gray')
ax.set_ylim(S0.max()*1e-9, S0.max()*3)
ax.set_xlabel('λ [nm]'); ax.set_ylabel('L_λ [arb. units]'); ax.legend(); ax.grid(alpha=.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('q1_spectrum.png', dpi=140)

# --- (c) mass budget and (d) luminosity budget: closed-form power-law integrals
I = lambda a, b, p: (b**(p+1) - a**(p+1)) / (p+1)        # ∫_a^b M^p dM
for cut in (1, 8):
    fm = I(M_LO, cut, 1-ALPHA) / I(M_LO, M_HI, 1-ALPHA)
    fl = I(cut, M_HI, A_L-ALPHA) / I(M_LO, M_HI, A_L-ALPHA)
    print(f"M<{cut}: {fm:.1%} of the mass;  M>{cut}: {fl:.2%} of the light")

# --- (d) luminosity function dN/dL = ξ(M) dM/dL ∝ L^((1-α)/a_L - 1)
L = np.logspace(np.log10(M_LO**A_L), np.log10(M_HI**A_L), 300)
M = L**(1/A_L)
dNdL = M**-ALPHA * (1/A_L) * L**(1/A_L - 1)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 3.4))
ax[0].loglog(L, dNdL); ax[0].set_xlabel('L/L☉'); ax[0].set_ylabel('dN/dL'); ax[0].set_title(f'luminosity function, slope {(1-ALPHA)/A_L-1:.2f}')
ax[1].loglog(L, L*L*dNdL); ax[1].set_xlabel('L/L☉'); ax[1].set_ylabel('L²·dN/dL'); ax[1].set_title(f'light per ln L, slope +{(1-ALPHA)/A_L+1:.2f}')
for a in ax: a.grid(alpha=.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('q1_lf.png', dpi=140)

Why the peak sits at the hot end. In the integrand, the IMF ($M^{-2.35}$) loses to the radius ($M^{1.6}$) times the steep growth of $B_\lambda$ with $T$ on the blue side. Per unit $\ln M$, light goes as $M\cdot\xi\cdot L\propto M^{2.15}$ — strongly rising — so the most massive stars set the shape.

It is not one blackbody. The long-wavelength side falls more slowly than a single 43 000 K Planck curve because the cooler stars add red light there. A population spectrum is always broader than any one star's.

(b) The same population 10⁹ years later

Findwhich stars survive, and the new spectrumGiven$t=10^9$ yr; lifetime (F.6); ignore everything off the main sequence

A star is still on the main sequence if its lifetime exceeds the age. Because lifetime falls with mass, "survivors" means "everything below one turnoff mass": so (b) is (a) with the upper integration limit moved down.

Where (F.6) comes from (one line, so you see why the exponent is −2.5): fuel ∝ $M$, burn rate = $L\propto M^{3.5}$, so

$$t_{MS}\propto\frac{M}{L}\propto\frac{M}{M^{3.5}}=M^{-2.5},\qquad t_{MS}(M_\odot)\approx10^{10}\ \mathrm{yr}$$

Set lifetime = age and solve for the turnoff mass:

$$10^{10}\,M_{TO}^{-2.5}=10^{9}\ \Longrightarrow\ M_{TO}^{2.5}=10\ \Longrightarrow\ M_{TO}=10^{1/2.5}=10^{0.4}$$

Evaluate: $M_{TO}=2.51\,M_\odot$, whose temperature (common step) is $T=5772\times2.51^{0.475}\approx8\,900$ K — an A star.

Recompute the spectrum with the upper limit replaced (same integrand as (a)):

$$L_\lambda^{\rm pop}(t=10^9\,\mathrm{yr})\propto\int_{0.1}^{2.51}M^{-0.75}B_\lambda\big(T(M)\big)\,dM$$

Compare to (a). The peak moves from 67 nm to ≈385 nm (Wien temperature ≈7 500 K, a bit below the turnoff star's 8 900 K because cooler stars pile up on the red side). Bolometric luminosity, from the closed-form light integral of part (d):

$$\frac{L(t)}{L(0)}=\frac{\int_{0.1}^{2.51}M^{1.15}dM}{\int_{0.1}^{100}M^{1.15}dM}=\frac{2.51^{2.15}-0.1^{2.15}}{100^{2.15}-0.1^{2.15}}\approx3.6\times10^{-4}$$

while the mass still on the main sequence is $\int_{0.1}^{2.51}M^{-1.35}dM\big/\int_{0.1}^{100}M^{-1.35}dM=74\%$ of the original.

$$\boxed{M_{TO}=10^{0.4}\approx2.5\,M_\odot;\ \text{the spectrum is truncated at }T\approx8\,900\ \mathrm{K},\ \text{peaks near }385\ \mathrm{nm},\ L\ \text{drops}\sim3000\times}$$

Light is fragile, mass is not. After 1 Gyr the population kept 74% of its mass in main-sequence stars but only 0.04% of its main-sequence light. In reality giants and remnants (ignored here) add light back, mostly in the red — that's why the question tells you to ignore them.

The cut-off is visible as a sharp drop on the blue side: no star hotter than 8 900 K is left, so nothing is emitted below ~100 nm.

(c) Who carries the mass?

Findfraction of total mass in $M\lt 8$ (and $M\lt1$) versus aboveGiven(F.1), $0.1\le M\le100$

The mass in a bin is (number) × (mass per star) = $M\,\xi(M)\,dM$. That is a power law, so everything is a one-line integral and the sign of the exponent tells you which end wins.

Write the mass integral:

$$\mathcal M(M_a,M_b)=\int_{M_a}^{M_b}M\,\xi(M)\,dM\propto\int_{M_a}^{M_b}M^{1-2.35}dM=\int_{M_a}^{M_b}M^{-1.35}dM$$

Integrate ($\int M^p dM=M^{p+1}/(p+1)$, $p=-1.35$):

$$\mathcal M(M_a,M_b)\propto\frac{M_b^{-0.35}-M_a^{-0.35}}{-0.35}=\frac{M_a^{-0.35}-M_b^{-0.35}}{0.35}$$

The $1/0.35$ and the IMF normalisation cancel in every ratio below.

Evaluate the powers once: $0.1^{-0.35}=2.239,\ 1^{-0.35}=1,\ 8^{-0.35}=0.483,\ 100^{-0.35}=0.200$.

Cut at 8 M☉:

$$\frac{\mathcal M(0.1,8)}{\mathcal M(0.1,100)}=\frac{2.239-0.483}{2.239-0.200}=\frac{1.756}{2.039}=0.861$$

Cut at 1 M☉:

$$\frac{\mathcal M(0.1,1)}{\mathcal M(0.1,100)}=\frac{2.239-1}{2.039}=0.607$$

Check the dependence on the lower limit, since $M^{-0.35}$ grows as $M\to0$ — the low end is where the mass integral is sensitive:

$M_{\rm low}$mass in $M\lt1$mass in $M\lt8$
0.0864%87%
0.161%86%
0.526%74%
$$\boxed{\text{Low-mass stars dominate the mass: }86\%\text{ in }M\lt8\,M_\odot,\ 61\%\text{ in }M\lt1\,M_\odot\ (\text{for }0.1\text{–}100\,M_\odot)}$$

The exponent rule. $\int M^{p}\xi\,dM\propto M^{p+1-\alpha}$. Number: $1-\alpha=-1.35$; mass: $2-\alpha=-0.35$; light: $3.5+1-\alpha=+2.15$. Negative → the lower limit dominates; positive → the upper limit dominates. Mass sits close to zero (−0.35), which is why it's spread out and sensitive to $M_{\rm low}$.

By number the result is extreme: 99.7% of stars are below 8 M☉ and 95.5% below 1 M☉.

(d) Luminosity function, and who carries the light

Find$\Phi(L)=dN/dL$; fraction of total light from low vs high massGiven(F.1), (F.2)

The luminosity function counts the same stars as the IMF, just binned in $L$ instead of $M$. A change of variables does it: the stars in $dM$ are the stars in the corresponding $dL$.

Conserve the number of stars between the two binnings:

$$\Phi(L)\,dL=\xi(M)\,dM\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Phi(L)=\xi\big(M(L)\big)\left|\frac{dM}{dL}\right|$$

Invert (F.2) and differentiate:

$$L=M^{3.5}\ \Rightarrow\ M=L^{1/3.5},\qquad \frac{dM}{dL}=\frac{1}{3.5}L^{1/3.5-1}$$

Substitute both into $\Phi$:

$$\Phi(L)\propto\big(L^{1/3.5}\big)^{-2.35}\cdot\frac{1}{3.5}L^{1/3.5-1}=\frac{1}{3.5}L^{-2.35/3.5+1/3.5-1}$$

Collect the exponent:

$$-\frac{2.35}{3.5}+\frac{1}{3.5}-1=\frac{1-2.35}{3.5}-1=-0.386-1=-1.386$$ $$\Phi(L)\propto L^{-1.39},\qquad 10^{-3.5}\lesssim L/L_\odot\lesssim10^{7}$$

Total light per luminosity bin is $L\,\Phi(L)\,dL$. On a log axis (equal $d\ln L=dL/L$) that is

$$\frac{dL_{\rm tot}}{d\ln L}=L^2\Phi(L)\propto L^{+0.614}$$

rising — each decade of brighter stars contributes ~4× more light than the one below ($10^{0.614}=4.1$).

Same answer in mass (closed form, as in (c)): light in a mass bin is $L\,\xi\,dM\propto M^{3.5-2.35}dM=M^{1.15}dM$, so

$$\frac{L(M\gt8)}{L_{\rm tot}}=\frac{100^{2.15}-8^{2.15}}{100^{2.15}-0.1^{2.15}}=1-\frac{87.4}{19\,953}\approx0.9956$$

and above 1 M☉: $1-1/19\,953=0.99995$.

$$\boxed{\Phi(L)=\frac{dN}{dL}\propto L^{\frac{1-\alpha}{3.5}-1}=L^{-1.39};\quad \text{massive stars dominate the light: }99.6\%\text{ from }M\gt8\,M_\odot}$$

Trap: "per unit L" vs "per log L". $L\,\Phi(L)\propto L^{-0.39}$ falls, which looks like "faint stars win". But that is light per unit $L$, and the bright bins are enormously wide. Per decade ($L^2\Phi$) or integrated, bright stars win. The integral is what the question asks.

Parts (c) and (d) together are the punch line of the IMF: low-mass stars hold the mass, high-mass stars make the light. That's why a galaxy's light is a poor tracer of its stellar mass, especially if it recently formed stars.

(e) Ionizing hydrogen

Findthe threshold wavelength; which stars supply the ionizing photonsGiven$\chi_H=13.6$ eV (F.8)

A photon ionizes ground-state hydrogen if its energy is at least 13.6 eV; converting energy to wavelength gives a maximum wavelength. Then ask which blackbodies emit a meaningful share of photons short of it.

Invert $E=hc/\lambda$ (F.8):

$$\lambda_{\max}=\frac{hc}{\chi_H}=\frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8})}{13.6\times1.602\times10^{-19}}\ \mathrm{m}=9.12\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m}$$

Handy shortcut: $hc=1240$ eV·nm, so $1240/13.6=91.2$ nm.

Which temperature puts its peak there? From (F.7):

$$T=\frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{91.2\times10^{-9}}\approx3.2\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{K}$$

— O-type and the earliest B-type stars. Cooler stars only reach 91.2 nm in their exponential Wien tail.

Quantify the tail. Fraction of a blackbody's photons with $\lambda\lt91.2$ nm (numerical integral of the Planck photon spectrum, using $T(M)$ from the common step):

$M\ [M_\odot]$1102050100
ionizing fraction$4\times10^{-10}$0.5%3.4%17%35%

Weight by the IMF. Integrating (ionizing photons per star) × $\xi(M)$ over the population: stars above 8 M☉ produce essentially 100% of the ionizing photons and stars above 20 M☉ produce 99.6% — while they are only 0.07% of the stars by number.

$$\boxed{\lambda\le\frac{hc}{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}=91.2\ \mathrm{nm};\ \text{ionization is done by the hot, massive O/early-B stars }(T\gtrsim3\times10^4\ \mathrm{K})}$$

Why HII regions mark young star formation. The ionizing stars are exactly the ones with lifetimes of a few Myr (F.6: $t_{MS}(30\,M_\odot)\sim2\times10^{6}$ yr). Once they die the ionizing output collapses — set the age slider in (a) to log t = 7: the share of light below 91.2 nm falls from 43% to 2.6% of a total that is itself 50× fainter, a ~1000× drop in 10 Myr. So Hα/HII emission traces star formation of the last ~10 Myr.

Model note: with this simple $R\propto M^{0.8}$ the 32 000 K threshold falls at ~36 M☉; real O stars reach it at ~20 M☉. The conclusion is the same.

Question 2

Gravitational microlensing light curves

(a) A microlensing light curve arises when a distant star passes close to the line of sight of a nearer star. Write a program that takes the relevant parameters (star positions/distances, velocity, lens mass, etc.) and plots the magnification as a function of time (Maoz eqs. 6.28–6.29 and the figure above them; also at the end of tutorial 3). (b) Food for thought: how does a planet orbiting the lens star affect the light curve, and why?

(a) A program for the point-lens light curve

Find$A(t)$ from physical inputs $M,\ D_l,\ D_s,\ v,\ b$Given(F.9)–(F.11). Example: $M=0.3\,M_\odot$, $D_l=4$ kpc, $D_s=8$ kpc (bulge source), $v=200$ km/s, closest approach $b=0.5$ AU in the lens plane

Everything in microlensing is measured in units of the Einstein radius. So the program does three things: compute $R_E$ from the physics, turn the straight-line motion into $u(t)$ in those units, and feed $u(t)$ into $A(u)$.

Einstein radius from (F.9), with $D_{ls}=D_s-D_l=4$ kpc:

$$R_E=\sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\,\frac{D_lD_{ls}}{D_s}}=\sqrt{\frac{4(6.674\times10^{-11})(0.3\times1.989\times10^{30})}{(2.998\times10^8)^2}\cdot\frac{(4)(4)}{8}\,\mathrm{kpc}}$$

Evaluate. $4GM/c^2=1.77\times10^{3}$ m and $D_lD_{ls}/D_s=2$ kpc $=6.17\times10^{19}$ m, so

$$R_E=\sqrt{1.77\times10^{3}\times6.17\times10^{19}}\ \mathrm{m}=3.31\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{m}=2.21\ \mathrm{AU},\qquad \theta_E=\frac{R_E}{D_l}=0.55\ \mathrm{mas}$$

Einstein crossing time — the event's only clock:

$$t_E=\frac{R_E}{v}=\frac{3.31\times10^{11}}{2\times10^{5}}\ \mathrm{s}=1.65\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{s}=19.1\ \mathrm{days}$$

Turn the motion into $u(t)$. The source moves on a straight line relative to the lens. Decompose its offset into the perpendicular part (fixed, $u_0=b/R_E$) and the along-track part ($(t-t_0)/t_E$); Pythagoras gives (F.10):

$$u_0=\frac{0.5\ \mathrm{AU}}{2.21\ \mathrm{AU}}=0.226,\qquad u(t)=\sqrt{u_0^2+\left(\frac{t-t_0}{t_E}\right)^2}$$

Magnification (F.11), maximal at closest approach $u=u_0$:

$$A_{\max}=A(u_0)=\frac{0.226^2+2}{0.226\sqrt{0.226^2+4}}=\frac{2.051}{0.226\times2.013}=4.51$$

Code it. einstein_radius(), magnification(), and light_curve() below implement exactly these three lines; the plot shows several impact parameters.

$$\boxed{A(t)=\frac{u^2+2}{u\sqrt{u^2+4}},\ u=\sqrt{u_0^2+\big(\tfrac{t-t_0}{t_E}\big)^2};\ \text{example: }R_E=2.21\ \mathrm{AU},\ t_E=19.1\ \mathrm{d},\ A_{\max}=4.51}$$
microlens.py — the Q2 program
"""HW3 Q2 - point-lens microlensing light curve (Maoz eqs. 6.28-6.29)."""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G, c = 6.674e-11, 2.998e8
M_SUN, KPC, AU, DAY = 1.989e30, 3.086e19, 1.496e11, 86400.0

def einstein_radius(M_sun, D_l_kpc, D_s_kpc):
    """R_E in the lens plane [m]:  R_E² = (4GM/c²) · D_l D_ls / D_s"""
    D_l, D_s = D_l_kpc*KPC, D_s_kpc*KPC
    return np.sqrt(4*G*M_SUN*M_sun/c**2 * D_l*(D_s - D_l)/D_s)

def magnification(u):
    """A(u) = (u² + 2) / (u √(u² + 4))"""
    return (u**2 + 2) / (u*np.sqrt(u**2 + 4))

def light_curve(M_sun=0.3, D_l_kpc=4.0, D_s_kpc=8.0, v_kms=200.0, b_AU=0.5, t0_day=0.0, span=4.0):
    R_E = einstein_radius(M_sun, D_l_kpc, D_s_kpc)
    t_E = R_E / (v_kms*1e3) / DAY                     # Einstein crossing time [days]
    u0 = b_AU*AU / R_E                                # impact parameter in R_E units
    t = np.linspace(t0_day - span*t_E, t0_day + span*t_E, 2000)
    u = np.sqrt(u0**2 + ((t - t0_day)/t_E)**2)       # straight-line relative motion
    return t, magnification(u), t_E, u0, R_E

Limits. $u\to0$: $A\approx1/u$ (diverges for a point source; a real star's finite disk caps it). $u\gg1$: $A\approx1+2/u^4$, so the event switches off fast outside the ring. $u=1$: $A=3/\sqrt5=1.34$ — why the "Einstein radius" is the natural detection scale.

Signatures that make it microlensing, not a variable star: symmetric in time, achromatic (gravity bends all colors equally), and it happens once. Degeneracy: $t_E\propto\sqrt{M D_lD_{ls}/D_s}/v$ is the only physical number in a point-lens curve, so mass, distance and speed come out only in combination.

(b) A planet around the lens

Findhow the light curve changes, and whyGivenplanet/star mass ratio $q=m_p/M\ll1$, projected separation $s$ (units of $\theta_E$)

The planet is a second, tiny lens. It does almost nothing on its own — but during the event the star's lens makes two images that sweep along the Einstein ring, and if one passes near the planet, the planet lenses that image again. The result is a short, sharp blip on top of the smooth curve.

The planet's own Einstein radius scales as $\sqrt{\text{mass}}$ (F.9, same distances):

$$\theta_{E,p}=\sqrt{q}\,\theta_E\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t_{E,p}\approx\sqrt{q}\,t_E$$

Size the blip. Jupiter-like planet around the 0.3 M☉ lens, $q\approx10^{-3}$: $t_{E,p}\approx\sqrt{10^{-3}}\times19.1\ \mathrm{d}=0.6\ \mathrm{d}\approx14$ h. Earth-like, $q\approx10^{-5}$: ~1.5 h. The anomaly lasts hours to a day inside an event lasting weeks.

Why it only works near the ring. The star's images sit at $\theta_\pm=\tfrac12(u\pm\sqrt{u^2+4})\theta_E$ (F.12) — always near $\theta_E$ while the event is bright. The planet can only perturb an image it is close to, so planets at $s\approx0.6$–$1.6\,\theta_E$ (the "lensing zone", a few AU here) are the detectable ones.

Bright spike or dip? A planet near the bright outer image ($s\gt1$) creates a planetary caustic on the planet side; a source crossing it gives a sharp spike (often a pair of caustic-crossing jumps). Near the faint inner image ($s\lt1$) the planet tends to destroy flux — a dip. In high-magnification events ($u_0\ll1$) the source passes the tiny central caustic at the peak, so the peak itself is distorted: the most sensitive configuration.

Is the planet moving? Its orbital period (years) is much longer than $t_E$ (weeks), so the two-body lens is effectively static during the event — the anomaly is set by geometry, not by the orbit.

$$\boxed{\text{A short }(\sim\sqrt{q}\,t_E)\text{ spike or dip, asymmetric, when a lensed image passes the planet near }\theta_E;\ \text{its timing gives }s,\ \text{its duration gives }q}$$

Why microlensing finds planets others can't. The signal needs no light from the planet or even the host — only its mass — and it peaks for planets at a few AU around faint, distant M dwarfs, exactly where transits and radial velocities are weakest. The price: each event happens once, so the blip must be caught by round-the-clock monitoring.

Point-source approximation: a real source star has a finite disk (~0.001–0.01 θ_E), which rounds off caustic spikes. For Earth-mass planets this smearing can be comparable to the blip itself.
