# Chapter 7 (The observer's toolkit, missions 7.1 `night` and 7.2 `photometry`): audit

## Summary
- Both missions can be finished through the UI alone after the earlier missions (`H.open({before: id})`). Neither has `needs` or `unlocks`, and neither uses the axis menus, so there are **no locked-axis problems** in chapter 7.
- The astronomy is right. Sidereal time agrees with astropy to 0.3 s, the Sun to 0.01°, sunset and −18° twilight to the minute, and M42 transits to 1 min. Target altitudes are off by up to 0.4° because J2000 coordinates are used without precession.
- Worst problems: (1) the night planner's ResizeObserver is never stopped, so once 7.1 has been played, **7.2 opens with the sky forced into the horizon frame, pointing at M42**; (2) the sky's own date/time box and *Now* button go out of step with the planner, which ignores them; (3) the arrow keys cannot move any instrument slider; (4) 7.2 is done with three clicks at the default aperture, so the curve-of-growth lesson can be skipped; its result text says "S/N of only a few" when the screen shows S/N ≈ 50.
- Top 3 improvements: make 7.1 test its own prediction (fix the time at 00:00 and look for the month); start 7.2 with a 2 px aperture and use a star away from the image edge as "star A"; add the Moon and the twilight stages to the night plot (the slides' STARALT plots show both).
- Bugs: 0 blocker, 2 major, 7 minor, 4 cosmetic.

## Mission by mission

### 7.1 `night`: Planning a night
- **Can it be finished through the UI?** Yes. Through the UI: lecture (6 beats) → predict (December) → drag the Date slider to 14 Dec and set Local time to 23:30 → "done Altitude 53.9° of a possible 54.0°". Nothing is locked: the mission has no `needs` and uses no axis menus.
- **Numbers checked against astropy 8.0** (`work/ch7/check_astropy.py`, `astro_js.js`): GMST within 0.3 s on 6 dates. Sun RA/Dec within 0.007°. Sunset/sunrise and −18° twilight on 1 Jul, 22 Sep, 15 Dec, 20 Mar and 21 Jun within 1 min (for example 15 Dec: sunset 16:38, dark 18:08–05:06, UTC+2). M42 transit within 1 min (15 Dec 23:39; transit at 00:00 near 6 Dec, so the answer "December" holds). Target altitudes within 0.4°: J2000 RA/Dec are used with sidereal time of date and no precession (26 yr × 50″). This is harmless here, but `A.equToHor` should precess if the horizon frame is ever used for precise work.
- **Task window**: on 1 Jan the task is done from 22:00 to 23:00 (±0.6 h around transit at 22:31); at 22:00 the azimuth is 167°. Fair.
- **Lecture**: accurate and compact; the arithmetic is right (f/5, ×40, (200/7)² = 816 ≈ 7.3 mag; Hubble 0.058″, Keck 0.055″; sidereal day 23 h 56 m 04 s; X = 5.6 at 10° → 11.18; GC h_max 30.4°). Small points: "The sky on the left" (it is the middle pane on desktop); Orion α "≈ 5.5 h" in the lecture against "5.6 h" in the explanation (M42 = 5.59 h).
- **Exercise design**: the prediction asks "which month is M42 highest **at midnight**", but the task accepts any dark hour in Oct–Feb (Feb 15 at 19:30 passes). The verdict "The data say: December" is fixed and the student never checks it. Suggestion: first fix the time at 00:00 and find the date when it is highest (done = within ±10 days of 6 Dec), then the dark-sky part.
- **Explain & practice**: correct (54°; 84.1°; 0.315 mag; VLT 0.0169″). Answers rounded as in the solutions (84, 0.32, 0.017) are accepted.
- **Layout**: at 1600×950 everything fits. At 1100×800, B13. At 390×844 it is usable: plot, then target, sliders and readout; no horizontal scroll. The two near-identical gold curves (B9) are worst on the phone. Leaving for 7.2 and coming back reopens 7.1 at its lecture, as expected; B1 is the only state that leaks.
- **Views**: the horizon dome follows the target well; the Sun and twilight colour the sky. Missing: the Moon (phase and position; the slides' STARALT plots show it), the civil/nautical/astronomical stages in the plot, a line at h_max, any picture of a telescope or of the Wise Observatory.

### 7.2 `photometry`: Counting photons
- **Can it be finished through the UI?** Yes, too easily (B4): with the default aperture, clicking A, B, C gives "done 3 of 3". Nothing is locked.
- **Numbers**: re-measured in Python (`work/ch7/phot.py`). The code's `measure()` reproduces SDSS within 0.1 mag for r ≥ 5 px on A, B, C, D and F. The S/N peaks at r ≈ 2.5–3 px (0.65–0.8″ ≈ 0.6 FWHM), as the explanation says ("about the size of the seeing"). Seeing 1.20–1.29″. Star E's magnitude drifts to 21.53 at r = 15 px (a neighbour gets in). The noise model is the sky only (σ_sky √N_pix). The lecture's S/N equation also counts the star's own photons, so the bright stars' S/N (516, 419) are too high and "± 0.00" appears. Say "sky noise only" in the readout, or add the source term with an effective gain.
- **Lecture** (7 beats): correct and well paced (1 mJy → 16.40; nanomaggie = 3.631 μJy → 22.5; S/N 45 → 0.024 mag; R 2000 → 150 km/s; HARPS 2.6 km/s). The CCD explainer (bias/dark/flat/cosmic rays) is good. The first beat points the sky at the field with fov 4° in the drawn sky, where nothing is shown. In the exercise, the sky shows the ESO panorama blown up to 2°, a black blur (shot `phot-ex-ABC-default.png`).
- **Explain & practice**: correct (16.40; 25; 4×).
- **Layout**: fine at 1600×950 and 390×844: the image is 336 px wide on the phone, with a tap tolerance of about 15 screen px. At 1100×800 the aperture slider is below the window (B13).
- **Views**: the image, the markers and the curve of growth work. The mini plot has no axis numbers (you cannot read the best radius in px), and its legend text overlaps the S/N curve.

## Suggestions: simulations and interactive views
1. **Moon and twilight in the night planner** (extend `INS.night`, `observing.js:176`; add a low-precision `A.moonPos` to `astro.js`, Meeus ch. 47, about 40 lines). Plot the Moon's altitude and give its phase and distance to the target; shade civil, nautical and astronomical twilight in three tones; draw h_max and a transit tick; add an airmass axis on the right (X = 1, 1.5, 2, 3). The student sees why a full Moon near the target ruins a night. Misconception fixed: "dark = after sunset".
2. **A year on one page**: a date × hour heat map of the target's altitude, with the dark hours outlined (a new canvas in `INS.night`). The transit appears as a diagonal stripe moving 2 h a month; clicking a cell sets the sliders. Misconception fixed: "a star rises at the same time every night". Easy.
3. **Bouguer's line**: observe one standard star at five airmasses through a night (from the planner), then fit m against X in the lens to get k and m₀ (reuse the hand-fit tools in `instruments.js`). This is the real calibration from the slides (p73–76). Medium.
4. **Seeing and S/N** in 7.2: a seeing slider that convolves the real stars with a Gaussian (the PSF is already measured). The peak drops, the best aperture grows and S/N falls (slide p88). A pixel-scale slider shows under-sampling (p89). Medium, in `observing.js`.
5. **Celestial sphere explainer** (`explainers2.js`): a 3D sphere with horizon, equator, meridian and ecliptic. Drag the time: LST, hour angle and altitude change together; set the latitude to the pole or the equator. It fixes the confusion between RA/Dec and alt/az. Medium–hard.
6. **Filter and spectrum explainer**: Vega's spectrum and a flat f_ν source under the ugriz/UBVRI curves (SVO filter profiles; CALSPEC `alpha_lyr_stis`). Shows why AB − Vega is 0 in V and 1.9 in K, and, with a redshift slider on a galaxy template (the SDSS spectra from 9.1), the K-correction. Medium.
7. **Grating spectrograph explainer**: slit width, grooves per mm and order; see R and the overlapping orders, and how an echelle separates them with a cross-disperser (slides L9 p27–32). Medium.
8. **Exposure-time calculator**: S/N against t for a chosen magnitude, sky, read noise and aperture, showing where the source-limited √t regime meets the background-limited one. Easy, reuses the lecture's equation.

## Suggestions: new missions or exercises
1. **7.3 "Can you split it?"** (L8 resolution and seeing). Real data: the Washington Double Star catalogue (WDS, USNO), separations and magnitudes. The student chooses an aperture and a site's seeing and sees which pairs are resolved. Prediction: "Does a 10 m telescope split a 0.1″ pair from the ground?" (No, unless it has AO.)
2. **"Where is north?": solving the plate** (L8 WCS, L9 astrometric calibration). In the 7.2 image, click three stars and match them to their catalogue RA/Dec (already in `photfield.json`). The page solves for the pixel scale (0.262″/px) and the orientation. Easy.
3. **"Star or galaxy?"** (L9 p85). Measure FWHM and magnitude for every source in a larger Legacy Surveys DR9 cutout; the stars form a tight band at the PSF width. Check against the DR9 Tractor `TYPE` (PSF against REX/EXP/DEV). Real data, easy to fetch from legacysurvey.org.
4. **"Calibrate the night"** (L8 extinction, L9 standard stars): Landolt standard fields observed at several airmasses (simulated with k = 0.21 on real Landolt 2009 magnitudes), giving k and the zero point, then the magnitude of a target. Builds on suggestion 3 above.
5. **"Resolving power"** (L9 spectroscopy): a real high-resolution solar spectrum (for example the Kurucz/NSO solar flux atlas), degraded to R = 100–100,000. At what R does the sodium doublet (589.0/589.6 nm) split? Prediction plus slider; the answer is R ≈ 1000.
6. **"Light-travel time across the orbit"** (L8 HJD): eclipse times of a real eclipsing binary (for example from the TESS or Kepler light curves the game already uses) drift by ±8.3 min over the year until they are corrected to the Sun.
7. **Fix 7.1 to test its prediction**: lock the clock at 00:00 and have the student find the month when M42 is highest (done near 6 December). Keep the dark-sky window as a second step.
